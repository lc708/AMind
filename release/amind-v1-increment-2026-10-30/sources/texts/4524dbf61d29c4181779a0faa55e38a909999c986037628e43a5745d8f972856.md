Abstract

Scorpion stings are estimated to kill more than 3,250 people a year, with children the most severely affected. Here we evaluated whether Claude can design binders against medically relevant scorpion toxins. Working from an initial prompt and four follow-ups, Claude chose epitope residues on five sodium-channel toxins that we had selected (Cn2, Ts1, AaH II, AaH I, and LqhIII), generated 318,484 designs, and selected 180, to which we added 31 more with its help, for experimental characterization by Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience. Of the designs tested, 17 bound a toxin they were made against and met our quality criteria. Seven designs bound Cn2, four bound AaH II, four bound AaH I, two bound LqhIII (measured at one lab only), and none bound Ts1. The tightest Cn2 binder, Cn2-B1, had single-digit nanomolar affinity or better, and a competition assay showed that it competed with LR, an antibody fragment that targets Cn2 at its channel-binding site. Claude also tried to design the multi-toxin binders our prompt called for, but none of the 151 designs made against two or more toxins met our criteria against more than one. We have not yet tested whether any binder neutralizes its toxin, either in functional assays or in vivo. This work shows that Claude can design protein binders with potential therapeutic relevance for scorpion envenomation, suggesting an increasingly important role for AI systems in treating neglected diseases.

Introduction

Scorpions are estimated to sting more than 1.2 million people a year and to kill more than 3,250 victims, with children most impacted [1–4]. In the Buthidae, the family that includes most scorpions dangerous to humans [5], the most medically relevant toxins act on voltage-gated sodium (NaV) channels: 𝛽-toxins bind receptor site 4 and 𝛼-toxins bind site 3 [6]. Severe systemic effects arise downstream, from the neurotransmitters, including catecholamines, released in response [2], and it is these effects that a neutralizing binder must prevent. Most stings occur in resource-poor regions, where access to medical care is limited [7, 8]. In this report, Claude runs a binder-design campaign against these toxins with considerable autonomy, from choosing epitope residues to selecting designs to test.

Antivenoms generally rely on antibodies or antibody fragments from immunized animals. Treatments can work when given early [2, 9], but vary in composition [5, 8], are costly [2, 3], and are often limited in supply and in the venoms they recognize [10, 11]. Access differs by region: in Brazil and Mexico, antivenoms are produced domestically and reach victims through public health services [12–14], whereas cost and availability limit their use in North Africa, the Middle East, and rural India [3, 15, 16]. Recombinant antibody fragments and nanobodies against individual scorpion toxins have protected mice from those toxins and from whole venom [11, 17–20], with one human antibody fragment neutralizing an estimated 13 neurotoxins from nine Mexican species [21]. All of these came from immunized animals or libraries (Section S6), and to our knowledge, de novo designed binders against scorpion toxins have not been described.

Our approach was inspired by Vázquez Torres et al., who designed neutralizing proteins against three-finger toxins of elapid snakes [22]. Through de novo design and optimization, they produced high-affinity binders against a long-chain and a short-chain 𝛼-neurotoxin, with dissociation constants (𝐾D) of 1.9 nM and 0.9 nM, as measured by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Each binder fully protected mice when given at tenfold molar excess 15 min after three median lethal doses of its toxin. They argued that stable, easily made proteins could yield cheaper, more accessible antivenoms [22]. Nanobodies are also a promising modality for antivenoms. Ahmadi et al. immunized an alpaca and a llama with the venoms of 18 African elapid snakes. They combined eight of the resulting nanobodies into a defined mixture that, premixed with venom, prevented lethality in mice for 17 of the species [23]. More recently, Overath et al. targeted

Both design studies relied on experts to choose the target, epitope, tools, and filters. Generative tools [25–29] can now design binders from target structures [30], so these choices may be the bottleneck, and AI agents have begun to make them. In the Virtual Lab, agents guided by a researcher designed 92 nanobodies against SARS-CoV-2 variants, some of them functional [31], and agents have since designed binders against multiple targets [32–34]. Yet on 76 design tasks, agents beat fixed pipelines but trailed experts [35], and another agent was tested only computationally [36]. In our earlier report [37], Claude ran campaigns against 16 targets from a single prompt, but each design had a single target. The present study began when Citrini Research, a financial investment research firm, asked whether Claude could enable a group of motivated nonexperts to work on a genuine problem in human health [38]. Here we turned to the toxins of scorpion envenomation, which has been described as a neglected tropical disease [39]. Our prompt specified the targets, binding site, and design sets; listed candidate tools; and gave rules for novelty, scoring, and selection. Claude made the other choices autonomously (Methods).

We chose five toxins that span both toxin families (𝛼and 𝛽) and four regions of the world (Methods; Figure 1A). They are the 𝛽-toxins Cn2 (Mexico) and Ts1 (Brazil), the 𝛼-toxins AaH II and AaH I (North Africa), and the 𝛼-like toxin LqhIII (Middle East). Antibody fragments and nanobodies have been reported against Cn2, Ts1, AaH II, AaH I, and AahI′, a natural variant of AaH I that differs at one residue [18–20, 40, 41]. To our knowledge, none has been raised against LqhIII, although serrumab, a fragment that binds Ts1, is reported to reduce LqhIII’s effect on NaV channels [40] (Section S6). We asked whether Claude could design binders against the individual toxins, and whether a single design could bind several of them. Since the toxins belong to four different species of scorpions, envenomation victims would not be exposed to all five simultaneously. We therefore used them as the hardest test of multi-toxin design rather than as a required product profile. The practical need is regional: antivenoms must cover related toxins from several species, and binders that each address multiple toxins would enable a simpler cocktail. In Mexico, for example, a product has to cover Cn2, Css2, Cll1, Cll2, Cl13, and related Centruroides toxins [21]. Such binders are hard to design: the five toxins we selected share one fold but only 26 to 62% pairwise sequence identity. Only 14 aligned positions, eight of them cysteines belonging to the shared fold’s disulfide bonds, are identical in all five (Figure 1B,C).

Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience measured binding in three ways: 1) SPR at Adaptyv Bio with the design on the chip, 2) SPR at Twist Bioscience with the design captured on the chip as an Fc fusion, and 3) reverse biolayer interferometry (BLI) at both labs with the toxin on the sensor and the design in solution. Of the 211 designs tested, 17 bound one of four toxins, and none bound Ts1. The tightest Cn2 binder, Cn2-B1, has a single-digit nanomolar 𝐾D or tighter at both labs, and it competes with LR, an antibody fragment reported to neutralize Cn2 and whole venom in mice [18, 42, 43]. No design met our criteria for binding against more than one toxin, which could reflect Claude’s capabilities, our prompting, the available design tools, or the feasibility of such binders. The 17 binders have not been tested for neutralization in a cellular environment, so we do not claim to have produced an antivenom, but we consider this work an early step toward collaborating with AI systems to address envenomation.

Methods

Targets and epitopes

We chose five toxins using three criteria: 1) they come from species whose stings cause severe envenomation in Mexico, Brazil, North Africa, or the Middle East [1]; 2) they together cover both main families of scorpion toxins that act on NaV channels [6] (Figure 1A); and 3) they seemed obtainable as recombinant protein for binding assays.

From our prompt’s reading list, Claude chose published structures for four toxins [44–47] (Figure 1D). We found no published structure of AaH I, so Claude used predicted models (Table S9). We chose to target each toxin’s channel- binding site, defined as the toxin residues with a heavy atom within 5 Å of a channel. The site came from toxin-channel complexes of Cn2 (PDB 25IH) [44], AaH II (6NT4) [48], and LqhIII (7K18 [47] and 7XSU [49]; solid outlines in Figure 1E). Claude transferred the site by superposition from Cn2 to Ts1 and from AaH II and LqhIII to AaH I (dashed outlines), and added annotated residues to the sites of AaH II and Ts1 (Section S1.1). Before initiating design, Claude selected five amino acid hotspots in each site to target its designs against; some designs against multiple toxins were generated with other hotspot lists (Table S5; Section S1.1).

Design campaign

An internal research model, working within Claude Science, ran the campaign, starting from our prompt and with four follow-ups (Figure 2A,B; Section S2.1). Our prompt specified the targets, the design sets and their sizes, the rules for each design, the protein design and prediction tools, the scores and ranking formulas, and the novelty and diversity

rules. The follow-ups extended the campaign and mainly directed Claude toward multi-toxin binders. Claude chose the toxin structures and hotspots, how much to use each design tool, the score thresholds, the strategies for binding multiple toxins, and the fusions’ domain order and linker (Table S4). Claude produced a final list of 180 designs 64 hours after our initial prompt, and we later worked with it to add an additional 31 designs, making it a total of 211 designs.

Claude used 12 of the design tools listed in our prompt to generate backbones, or backbones and sequences: RFdiffusion [25], RFdiffusion3 [29], FreeBindCraft [27, 50], BoltzGen [28], PXDesign [51], Proteina-Complexa [52], Genie 3 [53], Mosaic [54], ESMFold2 Inversion [55], FoldCraft [56], BoltzDesign1 [57], and Protein Hunter [58]. For designing sequences on fixed backbones, Claude used SolubleMPNN [59] and SolubleCaliby, a variant of Caliby [60]. Claude then filtered out designs that resembled a toxin or a known protein, had cysteines or other sequence liabilities, made fewer than eight interface contacts, or were predicted as monomers with low confidence (Table S1). After testing, we compared all 211 designs with the five toxin structures, and none resembled a toxin in fold (Section S2.2).

Using three predictors (ESMFold2-Fast, ESMFold2 [55, 61], and Protenix v2 [62]), Claude co-folded each design with each toxin it was made against, and computed two scores for each predicted complex. The first, ipSAE [63], is a con- fidence score for the predicted interface. The second, self-consistency DockQ (scDockQ), is the DockQ score [64, 65] between the predicted complex and the design model, the complex that Claude kept as the design’s reference pose (Section S1.1). A design passed the score thresholds against a toxin if, with all three predictors, its ipSAE reached a threshold and its scDockQ was at least 0.23. Before any design was scored, Claude set an ipSAE threshold per toxin and per predictor. Each threshold was at least 0.15 and at least the 95th percentile of 28 to 31 decoys (unre- lated or scrambled proteins) and negative controls (Figure S2; Table S1). Claude generated 318,484 designs, screened 553,052 design-toxin pairs with one random seed of each predictor, and ranked 28,451 pairs with at least five seeds (Figure 2A).

Claude ranked the designs by the prompt’s formulas, principally a weighted z-score of the six scores. A design against multiple toxins was ranked by its lowest per-toxin value. Claude then selected designs under the prompt’s diversity rules, which capped the number from any one backbone, structural cluster, or design tool. In our fourth follow-up, we updated these rules for the all-five single domains to give the 30 places to those that passed the score thresholds against the most toxins (Sections S1.1 and S2.1).

The 211 tested designs fall into five groups (Figure 2C): 60 single-toxin designs (12 per toxin), 30 double 𝛽-toxin designs, 30 triple 𝛼-toxin designs, and 91 designs against all five toxins (59 single domains and 32 fusions of two domains). Each of the campaign’s 30 fusions joined a domain made against Cn2 and Ts1 to one made against AaH II, AaH I, and LqhIII through a rigid helical linker. In a second Claude Science session run in parallel to and after the campaign, we added 31 designs against all five toxins: 2 fusions and 29 single domains built on the two backbones of the campaign’s all-five single domains (Section S1.1).

Experimental characterization

Two contract research organizations (CROs), Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience, measured binding by SPR, injecting toxin from low to high concentration without regenerating the surface (Figure 3A). Adaptyv Bio expressed the designs cell-free, immobilized them, and tested each against the toxins it was made against (665 pairs), and seven designs against additional toxins (133 to 140 designs measured per toxin). Each pair had two to four replicates and up to eight concentrations from 0.3 to 1,000 nM. Some replicates against Cn2, Ts1, AaH II, and AaH I were measured by BLI instead. Twist Bioscience expressed each design as a human Fc fusion in HEK cells and captured the designs on an anti-human Fc chip. They then ran 210 of the 211 designs against Cn2, Ts1, AaH II, and AaH I at six concentrations from 4.1 to 1,000 nM, once per pair.

The two teams screened the designs against commercial recombinant Cn2, Ts1, and AaH II from different suppliers, one per lab (Table S11; Section S3.3). Adaptyv Bio also ran its screen against Cn2 and AaH II from Twist Bioscience’s supplier (Section S3.7). Adaptyv Bio expressed AaH I and LqhIII in-house, and Twist Bioscience’s supplier custom- made their AaH I and LqhIII. Twist Bioscience did not receive LqhIII in time, so only Adaptyv Bio measured binding against it. Our positive controls (Table S12; Section S3.4) were antibodies 9004G, LR, and RU1 (Cn2), serrumab (Ts1), 4C1 (AaH II), and 9C2 (AaH I) [18, 40, 66, 67].

We define the main screen as each lab’s SPR measurement of all designs. To test whether binding depended on which partner was immobilized, both teams ran reverse BLI, with the toxin on the sensor and the design in solution. Adaptyv Bio ran reverse BLI against all five toxins, and Twist Bioscience against Cn2, Ts1, AaH II, and AaH I.

A hit against a toxin alone was insufficient for us to declare binding, so after seeing the data we set four criteria for labeling a design a binder (Figure 3D). We define a binder of a toxin as a design with 1) a hit against the toxin at both labs, in every replicate, 2) every 𝐾D from the main screen at 1 µM or tighter, 3) a fitted maximum response (fitted 𝑅max) at Twist Bioscience within the theoretical 𝑅max (the largest response that one toxin molecule per binding site can give), and 4) no signal above background against CLEC12A in either of Twist Bioscience’s two SPR runs. We define the success rate of a group of designs as the share that we labeled binders.

Results

Interface scores fell as designs targeted more toxins

Even after selection on their in silico scores, multi-toxin designs scored lower than single-toxin designs, which, con- sistent with typical protein design campaigns, had very high scores. As measured with ESMFold2-Fast, the median ipSAE per toxin was 0.84 to 0.89 for single-toxin designs and 0.74 to 0.81 for double 𝛽-toxin and triple 𝛼-toxin designs. For the two groups against all five toxins, it was 0.39 to 0.78. The single-toxin designs had the highest median for every toxin and predictor, though many ranges overlapped (Figure 4). With the three predictors, the three antibody fragments against Cn2 [18, 66] scored 0.81 to 0.91, above every Cn2 decoy (at most 0.70). Fragments against the other toxins [40, 41, 67, 69, 70] separated less clearly from decoys (Table S26; Section S5.2).

Seventeen designs each met our criteria for binding against one of four toxins

Seventeen of the 211 designs tested met the criteria for binding: seven against Cn2, four against AaH II, four against AaH I, and two against LqhIII (Figure 5; Table 1). All 17 binders are predicted to be helical proteins without 𝛽-strands, comprising 16 single domains of 74 to 118 residues with three to seven helices and one 234-residue, two-domain fusion. In their predicted complexes, the binders each bury 1,656 to 2,739 Å2 and contact three to five of their toxin’s five hotspots (Table S17). Binders among the single-toxin designs contact more toxin residues than the other single-toxin designs (medians of 24 and 19), but this difference rests on only 11 binders (Section S3.11; Figure S16).

Of the 35 design-toxin pairs that were hits at both labs, 15 met all four criteria and 20 did not (Figure 3D; Tables S13 and S14). Of the 20 that did not meet all four criteria, 15 were ruled out due to a signal above background against CLEC12A. Of the rest, three had a 𝐾D above 1 µM against AaH II at Adaptyv Bio, and one exceeded the theoretical 𝑅max against Ts1. The last, design 9a8cdc7a against Cn2, was a hit in only one of three replicates at Adaptyv Bio and so did not meet criterion 1.

In reverse BLI, with the toxin on the sensor, all 17 binders were hits against their toxin at Adaptyv Bio. At Twist Bioscience, where we read the assay only as hit or no hit, 12 of the 15 binders tested were hits (6 of 7 against Cn2, 4 of 4 against AaH II, and 2 of 4 against AaH I). Against AaH II at Adaptyv Bio, 10 of the 11 other designs tested were not hits, including two of the three with a 𝐾D above 1 µM. Against AaH I, however, 19 of the 21 designs tested were hits (Section S3.10).

Ovalbumin is not among the four criteria, and requiring no hit against ovalbumin as well would leave 15 of the 17 binders (13 of the 15 pairs in Table S16).

Discussion

Of the 211 designs tested, 17 met our criteria for binding, including at least two of 12 single-toxin designs against each of four of the five toxins. Two Cn2 binders had single-digit nanomolar affinity or better by SPR at both labs. The neutralizing antibody LR, whose epitope overlaps the site we chose to target, competed with all Cn2 binders that could be assessed, whereas RU1, with a separate epitope, had little effect on the majority (Table S22). The final in silico score (Figure 2D) tracked success rates across design sets but did not pick out binders within a set (Section S5). In hindsight, the mean of its ipSAE terms did so modestly, in line with a meta-analysis of tested binders [73], but with so few binders an advantage over the final score could not be established.

Published antibody fragments and nanobodies remain the standard to beat for designed binders: three have reported sub-nanomolar 𝐾D values of 0.11 to 0.70 nM against Cn2 or AaH II [19, 21, 43], compared with approximately 3.1 nM at Adaptyv Bio and 1.7 nM at Twist Bioscience, both estimated to be upper limits, for Cn2-B1. Those against Cn2, AaH II, AaH I, and its variant AahI′ protected mice in challenge studies [18–20, 41, 42] (Table S27). Our success rate, 11 binders among 60 single-toxin designs (18%, 0 to 42% per toxin), is consistent with our earlier protein design campaigns [37].

Each of the 17 binders met our criteria against a single toxin (Figure 7). This could reflect Claude’s current capabilities, our prompting, the available design tools, or the biological feasibility of multi-toxin binders. Just 1 of the 91 all-five designs was a binder, but only against one toxin. The multi-toxin designs were also a limited test: the 59 all-five single domains share two backbones; the fusions joined domains that were not labeled binders as single domains or were never tested as such; and the 30 double 𝛽-toxin designs depended on Ts1, against which no design met the criteria (Section S1.1). In predicted complexes, typically fewer than half the positions a design contacts in every intended toxin carry the same residue, so one surface must accommodate different residues (Section S3.11). Predicted alone with ESMFold2, only 1 of the campaign’s 30 all-five single domains came within 2.0 Å of its design model, compared with 107 of the 120 designs against one to three toxins (Figure S28). Because 28 of those 30 share one backbone, this is close to a single case, but it suggests that selecting on co-folding scores against five toxins may favor binding surfaces that are not pre-formed. Two backbones gave three binders against a relative of the toxin they were first designed against, so backbones can transfer between related toxins. Other routes with precedent include binder cocktails [23, 74], consensus toxins as antigens [75, 76] and design targets [22], and linking proven binders, as in the two linked nanobodies that protected mice from scorpion venom [20]. Odin-Multi [77] and BindCraft2 [78] now also demonstrate multi-target binder design capabilities.

Limitations

• We measured binding but tested no design for neutralization in vitro or efficacy in vivo (Table S28). The toxins were recombinant proteins of undocumented activity, not toxin purified from venom. Positive controls worked at both labs for only Cn2 and AaH I (Section S3.4). • The affinities should be read as preliminary, since each 𝐾D rests on one concentration series at Twist Bioscience and on two to four fitted replicates at Adaptyv Bio (mean and standard deviation in Table S18). The SPR series were run without regenerating the surface, some show baseline drift, and surface effects could distort the rate constants (Section S3.6), so firmer values will need cleaner sensorgrams from an optimized assay with independent replicates. • The four AaH I binders meet the theoretical 𝑅max criterion at the toxin mass we derived from Twist Bioscience’s values, but not at the lower mass of the mature toxin (Table S10).

Future directions

Cn2-B1, which binds with single-digit nanomolar affinity or better and competes with the neutralizing antibody LR, is one of our strongest candidates to advance toward a therapy. We expect it to neutralize Cn2 in the same way as LR, by keeping the toxin from its channel, although this has yet to be confirmed experimentally. First, any binder that moves forward should be shown to bind toxin purified from venom, which would also reveal whether the absence of Ts1 binders reflects the designs or the recombinant toxin. Second, in patch-clamp recordings, the binder must stop a 𝛽-toxin from shifting NaV activation or an 𝛼-toxin from slowing inactivation without altering the currents itself [6]. Third, it should be tested in mice, premixed with toxin, then with venom, and finally given after venom, as published binders were [8, 79]. Multi-toxin binding can be tested without new designs, by measuring the 17 binders against close relatives of their targets. Beyond binding and neutralization, a treatment must also be safe, stable, affordable, and available where stings occur. Future campaigns could put such properties into the prompt as design goals alongside binding. Envenomation is clinically complex; reaching patients will also take the work of clinicians, funders, and policymakers.

In a single campaign, Claude designed binders against four of five scorpion toxins, including two Cn2 binders with single-digit nanomolar affinity or better, but binding multiple toxins with one design was unsuccessful. Our campaign relied on cloud compute and two CROs (Section S2.1), using on the order of 10,000 H100 GPU hours, and Claude went from our initial prompt to the final list of 180 designs in 64 hours with four follow-ups from us. Compute on that scale is more than most researchers can access, and we have worked to reduce that cost substantially. In separate work [80],2

Claude optimized more than 30 open-source biomolecular models, including the three structure predictors and most of the design tools used here, making structure prediction about 4 times faster on average with no measurable change in accuracy. With these models, a simplified prompt, and one GPU for 24 hours, Claude produced designs whose in silico scores were comparable to those of earlier campaigns that could use about 100 times as many GPU hours. That comparison rests on in silico scores alone, and the present campaign used neither advance, but future campaigns can draw on both.

We hope that this demonstration of Claude’s capabilities, and the 211 designs and binding data we are releasing, will help accelerate further research into antivenom design.

S1 Extended methods

S1.1 Design campaign

Target sites. For Cn2, the channel complex that defined the site includes the channel’s 𝛽1 subunit. Claude added residues from UniProt annotations to the sites of AaH II (K58, R62, and H64) and Ts1 (K12, W39, and W54), and chose five hotspots per toxin (Table S5). Some designs of Sets 6 to 8, which target multiple toxins, were generated with other hotspot lists. The sequence identities and C𝛼root-mean-square deviations (RMSDs) in Figure 1C come from pairwise global alignments (BLOSUM62; a gap of 𝑛residues costs 11 + (𝑛−1); end gaps free; among alignments of equal score, the one with the lowest RMSD).

Scoring. Each predictor received a toxin as its full mature chain, with the number of seeds as in Table S1. The design model is the complex of a design and a toxin that Claude kept as the reference pose for scDockQ. Every selected design came from one or more rounds of redesign on a design tool’s complex, a backbone remade by partial diffusion, or an earlier design’s predicted complex, so the design model can be such a prediction, and scDockQ then measures agreement with it. If the design model lacked an intended toxin, that toxin was superposed onto the toxin in the model before scDockQ was computed. In Set 8, nearly all designs on one backbone share one design model, so their scDockQ measures agreement with that shared pose.

Ranking and diversity. The ranking used three keys in order: 1) a complete set of seeds, 2) an scDockQ pass, and 3) the weighted z-score of the six scores. The diversity rules were: no repeated sequence; no two designs within five substitutions of each other; at most 2 designs from one backbone and 3 from one structural cluster in a set of 12, or 3 and 4 in a set of 30; at most half of a set from one design tool; and at least three tools per set. A rule could be relaxed stepwise if a set could not be filled, as in Set 7, where four backbones supplied four designs each. For designs made against multiple toxins, the z-score of the third key was the soft minimum −𝜏ln Σ𝑡exp(−𝑧𝑡/𝜏) over the intended toxins 𝑡, with 𝜏= 0.1. We removed the diversity caps for Set 8 in our fourth follow-up (Section S2.1). The spacing of at least 10 substitutions between designs was Claude’s own rule, and the final 30 designs keep it.

Sequence design and fusions. Multistate SolubleMPNN, which designs one sequence for several structures at once, generated 16 of the 30 designs in Set 7 and 28 of the 30 in Set 8, but none in Set 6 (Table S1). The fusions of Set 9 joined domains that had not been shown to bind on their own. In 29 of the 30, the first domain was one of six designs of Set 6, and none of the six was labeled a binder as a single domain. No second domain came from Set 7, and none was tested as a single domain.

Designs added later. Of the 31 designs of Set 10, 20 had passed the score thresholds against all five toxins in the campaign’s rounds of sequence redesign but were not selected. They are the only unselected designs we tested, so no unselected single-toxin design was tested. Nine are variants of 005ebdae and cc1c8edb, chosen by co-folding and molecular dynamics to improve binding against LqhIII, and two are fusions of those two designs, one in each domain order. None of the 31, and none of the 30 designs of Set 8, was a hit at either lab in the main screen.

S1.2 Experimental characterization

Designs. The 211 designs consisted of 179 single domains of 55 to 120 amino acid residues and 32 fusions of 174 to 234 amino acid residues. Table S2 shows how Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience produced them, and Table S3 how they measured them.

SPR at Twist Bioscience. Claude identified the hits from Twist Bioscience’s data under our direction, after Adaptyv Bio’s hits were known. A design was assessable if at least 50 response units (RU) of it were captured. The rule for hits in the Methods requires a response 5 robust standard deviations (SD) above the median, and one robust SD is 1.48 times the median absolute deviation of that toxin’s responses in the run. Responses 3 to 5 SD above the median were labeled weak or ambiguous. Against CLEC12A in the second run (the run against AaH I), responses about 2 to 3 SD above the median were labeled near-background. Neither label is a hit. Each response is the mean over about the last 12 s of an injection, minus the mean curve of that injection’s negative-control spots (trastuzumab; buffer; and 9C2 in the run against Cn2, Ts1, and AaH II). In the run against AaH I, each spot’s adjacent reference region had already been subtracted. The baseline was set once per series, without regeneration, so the response at 1 µM is cumulative. A baseline reset before each injection would count only the signal added at 1 µM, so Cn2-B1, which saturates at lower concentrations, would no longer be a hit.

SPR at Adaptyv Bio. SPR was run on a Carterra LSA-XT with CMDP chips. Strep-Tactin XT was amine-coupled to each chip at 50 µg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.5. Designs were captured through their twin-Strep tag for 750 s, followed by 600 s of buffer. In the main screen, the median capture of design spots ranged from 271 to 868 RU per run and was about 500 to 700 RU for each toxin, and Table S7 gives the binders’ capture levels. The running buffer was HBSTE (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, and 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.4), the flow rate was 50 µL/min, and the chip was held at 25 ◦C. Each series ran from the lowest to the highest concentration without regeneration. A replicate is one spot measured through one series, and the replicates of a pair did not always lie in separate runs: both replicates lay in one run for AaHII-B1 and AaHII-B2, two of the four for AaHII-B4, and two of the three for each LqhIII binder. Signals from reference spots with the same surface chemistry but no design, and from the leading buffer injection, were subtracted (double referencing), and a linear drift correction was made on a buffer segment.

S3.2 Adaptyv Bio’s fits and replicates

We fitted each pair’s single Twist Bioscience series with a 1:1 model (Section S1.2). Adaptyv Bio fitted all injections of each replicate together with their own pipeline, sharing one on-rate, off-rate, 𝑅max, and drift slope, with a bulk refractive-index offset for each phase (Table S8). A pair’s 𝐾D is the arithmetic mean over its fitted replicates, by SPR or BLI. For every binder, Adaptyv Bio selected the 1:1 model in the main screen. For five main-screen pairs, they selected a conformational-change model: a981802c and 6d0b4f3f against AaH I, and 33b2180d, 6d0b4f3f, and f8242edc against LqhIII, all fusions and none a binder. They did the same for 19 of the 29 reverse BLI replicates against AaH I. In these cases, we report their own 1:1 fits of the same measurements, marked approximate, so that all 𝐾D values come from one model. In reverse BLI against Cn2, Adaptyv Bio selected the 1:1 model for every design so that the values can be compared across designs, although, by their team’s account, some designs fit other models better.

Against AaH I, all 19 of Adaptyv Bio’s hits were hits, with a fit, in every replicate. The replicates of a pair differ by a median of 3.3-fold (1.1- to 16-fold), and those of the four binders differ 3.0- to 13-fold. The widest of these, for AaHI-B2, lies between two SPR replicates, so it is not a difference between SPR and BLI. For AaHI-B2, AaHI-B3, and AaHI-B4, the difference lies mainly in the on-rates (11- to 30-fold) and exceeds the gap between the labs’ values (1.1- to 2.0-fold), so these means are approximate (Table S18). S3.3 The toxins and the theoretical 𝑅max

Table S9 lists the five toxins and the structures used for design. Twist Bioscience’s three commercial toxins carry tags (Table S11), and their product sheets report purity by reducing SDS-PAGE and no test of activity. No control excludes binding to the His-SUMO tag of AaH II. The position of each tag matters, because many predicted interfaces reach the toxin’s C-terminus. In the predicted complexes (ESMFold2), 12 of the 17 binders, Cn2-B1 among them, have a heavy atom within 5 Å of the toxin’s C-terminal residue, and only Cn2-B2 has one within 5 Å of the N-terminal residue. The last residue of AaH II, H64, is a hotspot (Table S5), and the last four residues of Cn2 belong to its channel-binding site (defined in Section S4.1). Of the documented constructs, Ts1 carries a C-terminal tag at both labs, and AaH I and LqhIII carry one at Adaptyv Bio (Table S11).

For Cn2, 9 of the 12 single-toxin designs contact the C-terminal residue, and 4 of the 9 are binders. For Ts1, 11 of the 12 contact it, as do 28 of the 30 double 𝛽-toxin designs. The C-terminal tag could therefore have covered the designed site on Ts1 at both labs. Of the 6 binders against AaH I and LqhIII, however, 4 contact the toxin’s C-terminal residue in their predicted complexes, and all 6 were hits against these tagged constructs, so a C-terminal tag does not by itself prevent binding. Misfolding is a second possible explanation, since recombinant Ts1 refolded from E. coli inclusion bodies was reported to form a stable, disordered disulfide isomer [72]. Ts1 without a C-terminal tag, or purified from venom, would distinguish both explanations from designs that do not bind. Until then, the lack of Ts1 binders cannot be attributed to the designs, and so the result for the 30 double 𝛽-toxin designs cannot be either. Native Cn2, Ts1, AaH II, and LqhIII also end in a C-terminal amide, which recombinant proteins from yeast or E. coli are not expected to carry.

Whether a pair stays within the theoretical 𝑅max depends on the toxin’s mass. We used the masses recovered from the theoretical

𝑅max values that Twist Bioscience reported (Table S10). These equal the datasheet masses of Cn2 and Ts1; the mass of AaH II is above its datasheet mass and equals the mass on two distributors’ product sheets; AaH I has no datasheet. At the mature mass of AaH I, its four binders would exceed the theoretical 𝑅max, as would 9C2 (𝑅max ratio of 1.43 instead of 0.70). If 9C2 binds one to one, AaH I as used must weigh at least 9.7 kDa per binding site. At 9.7 kDa, AaHI-B2 is at 1.13 times the theoretical 𝑅max, and from 11.0 kDa all four binders stay within it. The 15 binders measured at Twist Bioscience reached 39 to 82% of their theoretical 𝑅max, assuming saturation within the injection, so such binders were detectable against Cn2, AaH II, and AaH I. Against Ts1, the limit for a hit left a binder a far narrower range, and all five hits exceeded the theoretical 𝑅max.

We also checked criterion 3 on Adaptyv Bio’s data, which the labels do not use. With the capture levels and the construct masses (Tables S7 and S11), we compared the response at the end of the 1 µM injection of each SPR replicate with the theoretical 𝑅max for one toxin per captured design. The 11 binders against Cn2 and AaH II reached 15 to 70% of it, and the six against AaH I and LqhIII reached at most 39%. By BLI, AaHI-B1, AaHI-B3, and AaHI-B4 reached 0.70 to 0.92 times the theoretical 𝑅max, and 9C2 reached 0.86 and 0.96. AaHII-B4 against Ts1 is the one pair that criterion 3 alone excludes at Twist Bioscience, and at Adaptyv Bio this single domain reached 2.4 times the theoretical 𝑅max on both SPR spots measured, which were in one run. This check was less strict than the one at Twist Bioscience. The capture level was recorded when the design was captured, so any design that left the surface afterward lowered the true limit below the one computed. We therefore did not use this check to label binders.

S3.6 Agreement between the two labs

The labs agreed more closely on hits than on affinities, with a Cohen’s 𝜅of 0.78 (interval 0.68 to 0.86) on the 533 pairs measured at Adaptyv Bio and assessable at Twist Bioscience. Of these, 53 were hits at either lab and 35 at both, and 10 of the other 18 were fusions. The affinities agreed within 10-fold for 28 and within 3-fold for 16 of the 32 pairs that were hits at both labs with a 𝐾D at both and no upper limit (Figure S6). Spearman’s 𝜌was 0.50 (95% interval 0.16 to 0.74, designs resampled).

Among the 15 binders measured at both labs, the gap between the labs mostly follows the toxin rather than the design (Figure S6). Adaptyv Bio’s 𝐾D was a median 1.7 times Twist Bioscience’s against Cn2 (6 pairs, 1.2- to 4.0-fold apart), 46 times against AaH II, and 0.54 times against AaH I (4 pairs, 1.1- to 4.1-fold apart). The gap against AaH II lies mainly in the association rates (Figure S10). These rates agree within 2.3-fold against Cn2, and Twist Bioscience’s are 10 to 19 times higher against AaH II. Against AaH I, Adaptyv Bio’s are 0.7 to 7.0 times Twist Bioscience’s. An apparent association rate scales with the share of toxin able to bind, so unequal active shares of toxin, or the designs’ different formats and surfaces, could produce such gaps. Against AaH I, the labs’ preparations differ, and for three of the four binders Adaptyv Bio’s own replicates differ more than the labs do (Section S3.2).

Three surface effects could also distort the rate constants, and we did not vary the flow rate or the surface density to test for them. Transport of toxin to the surface may limit the fastest association rates: in Cn2-B1’s two replicates at Adaptyv Bio, the fitted on-rate fell 3.6-fold as the top response rose 4.5-fold. Rebinding of released toxin may also slow the apparent off-rates, an effect that grows with surface density. If part of a tagged toxin is oligomeric, it could be held by avidity at either lab. The two copies on each Fc fusion could hold it at Twist Bioscience, and neighboring immobilized designs could do the same at Adaptyv Bio, more so at higher surface density. S3.7 SPR against toxins from a second supplier

Adaptyv Bio ran the designs by SPR against Cn2 and AaH II from Cusabio, the supplier of Twist Bioscience’s toxins (Table S11). Against AaH II, 4 of 132 were hits: the four binders (Figure S7). S3.8 The number of binders under other definitions

Table S16 gives the number of pairs that meet all criteria when the definition of a hit or of a criterion changes. Criterion 1 asks for a hit in every replicate. Counting a hit in any replicate would add 9a8cdc7a against Cn2, a hit in one of three replicates at Adaptyv Bio. Against LqhIII, the triple 𝛼-toxin design a1f798ca was also a hit in one of three replicates and has no reported 𝐾D, so it misses two criteria.

Claude also identified hits in Adaptyv Bio’s traces by a fixed rule like its rule for Twist Bioscience’s data. In at least half of a pair’s main-screen SPR replicates, the response over the last 10 s of association at 1,000 nM had to exceed the toxin’s median in that run by 5 SD (for the seven runs with fewer than 30 designs, the median and SD of a larger run against the same toxin) and to rise with concentration (Spearman’s 𝜌at least 0.8). The rule kept 43 of Adaptyv Bio’s 51 hits, including 14 of the 15 pairs that met all criteria, and added none. Its hits agreed with those from Twist Bioscience’s data (𝜅0.74, 95% interval 0.62 to 0.83) about as well as Adaptyv Bio’s own did (0.78).

The median of the 15 was 19 points, compared with +5 points (−8 to +18) at Twist Bioscience. A longer dissociation phase would show whether this remainder dissociates slowly or stays bound.

Cn2-B1 dissociated from Cn2 most slowly (Figure 6B): in each of its three SPR measurements, its signal fell by at most 17% over 8 to 10 min of dissociation. Its upper limit on 𝐾D uses no rate constant. It comes from the fraction of the top level reached at the lowest concentrations, counts every loss between injections as lost surface, and allows for drift. We set each spot’s zero at the start of the first injection and took 𝑅𝑖as the mean response over the last 10 s of injection 𝑖. With the top level 𝑅top as the highest 𝑅𝑖, reached at 𝐶top, and 𝑟𝑖= 𝑅𝑖/𝑅top, 1:1 binding gives 𝐾D ≤𝐶𝑖𝐶top(1 −𝑟𝑖)/(𝑟𝑖𝐶top −𝐶𝑖). This is an upper limit because, as concentrations rise on an empty surface, occupancy nears equilibrium from below. Both allowances loosen the limit. First, we counted all loss between injection 𝑖and the top level as lost surface, not released toxin: in place of 𝑅top, we used 𝑅𝑖plus the rise within each later injection, plus that injection’s length times the faster rate of fall over the last 390 s of the two adjoining dissociation phases. Second, we subtracted from 𝑅𝑖the highest level on a blank spot of the same run at that injection (0.2 and 8.0 RU at the lowest concentration). At Adaptyv Bio, the four injections up to 10 nM each gave a valid limit, and we took the smallest. At Twist Bioscience, the first injection (4.1 nM) gave the top level, so we used what the later injections added. With 𝜌𝑘the level at injection 𝑘over that at the first, 𝐾D ≤𝐶1𝐶𝑘(𝜌𝑘−1)/(𝐶𝑘−𝜌𝑘𝐶1), with both allowances (highest reference spot, 3.6 RU), and we again took the smallest.

Adaptyv Bio’s two replicates give 1.8 and 3.1 nM, and we report the looser of these; Twist Bioscience’s limit is 1.7 nM. Three other methods gave 0.064 to 2.2 nM at Adaptyv Bio: the largest off-rate the traces allow over the fitted on-rate, a stress test with the lowest on-rate they allow, and the limit above without its allowances. The first two gave 0.05 to 0.06 nM at Twist Bioscience, so the reported limits are the loosest at each lab. The limits assume that the toxin is at its stated concentration, and they hold for the sites that fill first, because a model with one class of sites does not fit the traces.

The two-lab criterion is conservative. It leaves out 13 pairs with a 𝐾D of 1 µM or tighter at the lab where they were hits and no hit against CLEC12A (Figure S9B); for 9 of them, CLEC12A gave background signal in both runs, as criterion 4 requires. For some, the other lab’s traces rise with concentration without reaching the level of a hit, most clearly for the fusion 40090fca against AaH I, which reached 14 to 56 RU in three replicates at Adaptyv Bio with slow dissociation. S3.10 Reverse BLI

In reverse BLI, the design is free in solution, so the result does not depend on how the design was immobilized. Adaptyv Bio measured 49 designs this way in 69 design-toxin pairs (Tables S19 and S20; Figures S11, S12, S13, and S14). All 17 binders were hits against their own toxin. Against Cn2, 12 of the 13 designs were hits, including 05efdf6e and ca06e722, which were hits at neither lab in the main screen. The exception, e32555c5, gave a weak signal that Adaptyv Bio’s team does not count as binding (0.30 nm at 1 µM; the three weakest hits, 700d103b, 05efdf6e, and 9a8cdc7a, gave 0.13 to 0.34 nm). Against AaH I, 19 of 21 were hits: the four binders and 15 other designs, 11 of them hits at both labs in the main screen. Against these two toxins, the assay thus did not separate binders from the other designs. Against AaH II, 5 of 15 were hits: the four binders and bbe37cf8, whose weak signal was not fitted. The other ten, Cn2-B1 among them, gave shifts of −0.06 to 0.04 nm at up to 1 µM. Seven of them had been hits against AaH II at one lab or both in the main screen. Here the assay agreed with our labels for 14 of the 15 designs, so the toxin on the sensor did not bind designs indiscriminately. Against LqhIII, 3 of 10 were hits (both binders and 443207a9), and four were unresolved. The sensors carried less LqhIII the higher the design concentration (1.07 to 1.28 nm at 31.6 nM but 0.05 to 0.29 nm at 1 µM, compared with 1.4 to 1.8 nm of AaH I throughout). At 1 µM, all ten designs gave 0.12 to 0.37 nm, so hits and other designs differed little in signal there. Against Ts1, all ten designs were unresolved. All 22 pairs with a result in two runs gave the same result in both.

The 𝐾D values are Adaptyv Bio’s 1:1 fits (Section S3.2). Cn2-B1 gave the tightest 𝐾D against Cn2, about 6.3 nM, and lost 3 to 8% of its signal over the 300 s wash. S3.11 Predicted interfaces and binding

We tested whether predicted interfaces separate binders from the other designs, using the ESMFold2 model of each design with each intended toxin (665 pairs). We fixed six comparisons before running them, with a threshold of 𝑝= 0.008: 1) toxin residues in contact, 2) buried area, 3) apolar share of the interface, 4) hotspots in contact, 5) design length, and 6) design tool. All 60 single- toxin designs had passed the same score thresholds, with ESMFold2 among the three predictors that selected them. Table S5 counts contacts differently (C𝛽atoms within 8 Å in the design models), so the two are not comparable. These analyses rest on predicted structures and on a small number of binders (11 and 6), and contacts and buried area are strongly correlated (Spearman’s 𝜌= 0.86 within toxins).

Among the single-toxin designs, binders had larger predicted interfaces (60 designs, only 11 binders; Figure S16). Medians were 24 versus 19 toxin residues in contact (rank-biserial 𝑟= 0.78, pooled within toxins; permutation 𝑝< 0.001) and 2,113 versus 1,666 Å2

of buried area (𝑟= 0.71, 𝑝< 0.001). Protenix v2 models show both differences (𝑟= 0.83 and 0.62). Without the Cn2 designs, however, neither difference passed the threshold (contacts, 𝑟= 0.65, 𝑝= 0.013; buried area, 𝑟= 0.57, 𝑝= 0.038). The other four comparisons did not separate the groups. Apolar share was lower in binders with both predictors, but the difference passed the threshold only with Protenix v2, and hotspots in contact, length, and design tool showed no difference.

Smaller predicted interfaces do not explain the lack of Ts1 binders. Of each toxin’s 12 designs, the binders and mean buried area were: Cn2, 5 and 1,999 Å2; Ts1, 0 and 1,890 Å2; AaH II, 2 and 1,792 Å2; AaH I, 2 and 1,624 Å2; and LqhIII, 2 and 1,492 Å2. No site property tracked these yields, whether net charge, site area and apolar share, aromatic hotspots, or mean buried area (𝑛= 5). In the predicted complexes, multi-toxin designs contact their intended toxins through overlapping surfaces whose toxin residues mostly differ, yet bury about as much area per toxin as single-toxin designs, or more (Table S21). Among the six binders designed against multiple toxins, the toxin they bound ranked first by final score in three and by buried area in one, compared with 2.2 expected by chance.

S4 Epitope and specificity assays

Two additional assays at Twist Bioscience tested where the designs bind Cn2 and whether they are polyreactive. Neither assay tests neutralization. Competition with reference antibodies, measured by BLI, covered 33 designs in 47 pairs with Cn2, Ts1, and AaH II, including 33 of the 34 pairs that were hits against these toxins at either lab (all but 925ee271 against Cn2). A separate run with 9C2 added 22 pairs with AaH I. Adaptyv Bio measured competition with LR and 9C2 in a different assay. Baculovirus particle (BVP) ELISA, for assessing polyreactivity, was run on the same 33 designs and, in a second run, on 18 more, the other designs that were hits against AaH I or LqhIII at either lab (Section S4.2). A control at Adaptyv Bio tested 52 designs in SPR against ovalbumin, an unrelated protein (Section S4.4).

S4.1 Competition with reference antibodies

Competition could be assessed for Cn2 and, in part, for AaH I. In tandem BLI (Table S3; Figure 3B), the toxin on the sensor was exposed to a reference antibody or to buffer and then to a design. The reference antibodies were 9004G, LR, and RU1 against Cn2; 4C1 against AaH II; serrumab against Ts1; and 9C2 against AaH I (Table S12). We assessed competition only where the antibody bound its toxin in the same assay. 4C1 did not bind AaH II, and serrumab did not bind Ts1. For LR and RU1, the assay worked as intended. Both bound the captured Cn2 (1.24 to 1.82 nm) and stayed bound in plain buffer (Figure S17A). When run as samples, each competed with itself but hardly with the other (Figure S17B), as expected for antibodies with separate epitopes. LR lost 96% of its signal after LR, and RU1 lost 87% after RU1, whereas RU1 lost 13% after LR, and LR lost 14% after RU1. The run with 9004G could not be assessed, because about half the captured Cn2 left the sensor in the 9004G wells before samples were added (Figure S18).

LR competed with all six Cn2 binders that could be assessed (87 to 95% of the signal lost), and five of the six lost little after RU1 (15 to 22%; Table S22; Figure S17C). RU1 binds a separate epitope, and in the crystal structure (PDB 4V1D) [18], LR and RU1 bind the same Cn2 molecule at once without touching each other (closest atoms 6.8 Å apart). RU1 therefore shows how much signal a design loses when an antibody of the same kind occupies another part of the toxin.

Cn2-B2 lost the most signal after RU1 (66%), and its predicted complex suggests why (Figure S19). We superposed the predicted complexes of the 16 designs tested against Cn2 on the crystal structure by the toxin and counted design atoms within 2.5 Å of RU1. With all three predictors, five residues of Cn2-B2 at a loop between two helices (D34 to P38; 32 atoms) overlap RU1’s light chain, and its footprint holds 6 or 7 of RU1’s 17 epitope residues (0 to 4 for the other designs; Table S22). In this pose, Cn2-B2 and RU1 cannot bind Cn2 at the same time, which suggests the assay told its pose apart from those of the other binders. Cn2-B1 overlaps RU1 only at its C-terminal end (R100 to A104; 14 atoms) and lost 16%, like designs without overlap; a free chain end may move aside more easily than a loop held between two helices. Cn2-B7 (signal too low to be assessed) has one such atom, and the other 13 designs have none. The models do not explain every loss, since e32555c5 (36%) and 8382cd8f (30%) lost signal after RU1 without predicted overlap.

S4.2 BVP ELISA

BVP ELISA tests for polyreactivity more broadly than SPR against CLEC12A, because baculovirus particles present many kinds of molecules at once. Twist Bioscience ran the assay twice, on 33 designs and on 18 more, with no design in both runs. Of the 51 designs, we excluded 3f0f990a, whose signal matched the wells without sample. The other 50 gave 4 to 108% (median 75%) of the signal of cixutumumab, the highest of Twist Bioscience’s three control antibodies on the same plates (palivizumab, 12 and 13% in the two runs; atezolizumab, 27 and 20%). The 32 designs of the first run gave 4 to 108% (median 51%), and the 18 of the second gave 34 to 106% (median 90%). Figure 6E shows the seven Cn2 binders, and Section S4.3 all 50. Twist Bioscience’s 12 reference nanobody-Fc fusions (VHH-Fc), run on another plate on the day of each run and scaled to that plate’s cixutumumab, gave 28 to 94% in the first run and 16 to 102% in the second. We compared each design with the range of its own run: 7 of the 50 designs fell below it, 38 within it, and 5 above it (second run: none, 17, and 1). Each design was read at one concentration: 0.07 to 0.13 mg/mL for 45 of the 50 and 0.02 to 0.05 mg/mL for the other five. We did not apply the assay’s published cutoff, a normalized score above 5 set on IgG [68], because Twist Bioscience’s readings are on a different scale.

BVP ELISA and SPR against CLEC12A flagged partly different designs. Of the 12 designs measured here that were hits against CLEC12A, 10 fusions and the single domain cac1ca4c gave 73 to 100%, whereas 05efdf6e, the strongest against CLEC12A, gave 8%. S4.3 Unintended binding

Signs of unintended binding were rare among single domains and more common among fusions (Figures S24 and S25). In the main screen, 301 pairs of a single domain with a toxin it was not made against could be assessed, all at Twist Bioscience. Of these, 2 were hits (0.7%) and 2 gave a weak or ambiguous signal (Figure S25C). With an intended toxin, 46 of 519 pairs were hits (8.9%), or 25 of 391 (6.4%) without the fusions. The first of the two hits, AaHII-B4 against Ts1, was found at both labs, but the signal exceeded the theoretical 𝑅max at Twist Bioscience, so it did not meet criterion 3 (discussed below). S4.4 SPR against ovalbumin

The control. The Adaptyv Bio team tested 52 designs in SPR against ovalbumin, an egg-white glycoprotein of 45 kDa [91] unrelated to the toxins. They were 48 of the 49 designs that were hits against any toxin at either lab (all but 925ee271), all 17 binders among them, and four others. As in the main screen (Section S1.2), the design was captured, here after purification, and ovalbumin was in solution, at 10, 31.6, 100, 316.2, and 1,000 nM. The concentrations rose without regeneration, so each injection started from the level left by the one before and the response at 1 µM is cumulative. Each design had two replicates over two runs, with two blank spots per run, and the designs’ median capture was about 700 RU in both runs, within the range of the main screen’s runs. Adaptyv Bio reported hit or no hit for each design and fitted no model. Their team advises against reporting a 𝐾D or binding strength from this control, because it gives hit or no hit only. The responses, and the shares of the theoretical 𝑅max derived from them, are our own readings of their traces. The runs included no positive control for ovalbumin, and the other 159 designs were not tested, so the rate of hits among designs that bind no toxin is unknown. We read all capture levels from the loading traces and left out the 3 of the 104 series whose spots held no design, so AaHI-B3, 6a9bcd5b, and 569c6be9 have one replicate each (Table S24; Figure S26). One more loading trace could not be read (4eae42fc, replicate 1), so that series is counted in RU only.

Hits. Eleven of the 52 designs were hits. Two were binders, Cn2-B1 and AaHI-B4, and nine came from the 35 other designs. At the end of the 1 µM injection, Cn2-B1 gave 246 and 254 RU (one replicate per run), and AaHI-B4 gave 181 and 210 RU (both replicates in one run). The other 15 binders gave 0 to 53 RU (median 19 RU), the nine other hits gave 78 to 734 RU, and the blank spots stayed at or under 5 RU. Replicates differed 1.4-fold at the median, and the response did not follow the capture level (Spearman’s 𝜌0.07 over 100 spots). The two LqhIII binders were not hits (12 to 22 RU); they were measured only at Adaptyv Bio and had CLEC12A at Twist Bioscience as their only other off-target control.

Size and shape. Ovalbumin is about four to five times heavier than the two toxin constructs compared here (Cn2, 9.6 kDa; AaH I, 11.2 kDa; Table S11), so an equal response in RU means far fewer molecules of ovalbumin. We therefore compared responses as shares of the theoretical 𝑅max for one molecule per captured design. At 1 µM ovalbumin, the share was 13% for Cn2-B1, 11% for AaHI-B4, 0.4 to 2.9% for the other 15 binders, and 7 to 41% for the nine other hits. Cn2-B1 reached the same share against Cn2 at 0.45 and 0.59 nM in its two SPR replicates, about 2,000 times less toxin than ovalbumin. AaHI-B4 reached the same share against AaH I at 52 nM in its SPR replicate, about 20 times less. Among the other hits, 9a8cdc7a reached its share (7%) against Cn2 only at 64, 290, and 400 nM in its three replicates, about 3 to 16 times less. In all 11 hits the response rose with every injection, showed no plateau, and kept 88 to 97% of its level through the 10 min wash. The data therefore give no 𝐾D. This shape is more typical of nonspecific adsorption than of binding at a defined site, but these data cannot tell the two apart.

What the response tracked. The response did not track the other specificity assays. Four of the 11 hits had a signal against CLEC12A at Twist Bioscience, and 12 of the 16 tested designs with such a signal were not hits against ovalbumin. With the BVP ELISA, 𝜌was −0.05 (50 designs). We compared the mean response at 1 µM with 25 measures of sequence, predicted structure, production, and the other assays (Figure S27), and three passed a Bonferroni limit of 0.002. They were the share of lysine and arginine (𝜌0.50), the number of lysines and arginines (0.43), and exposed lysines and arginines per surface area (0.42). The calculated isoelectric point fell just short (0.42), and measures of hydrophobicity ran the other way (−0.27 to −0.31). Ovalbumin is acidic (isoelectric point 4.5) [71], as are 16 of the 17 binders (4.0 to 4.8, calculated without tags from the EMBOSS p𝐾a values [92]). The exception is Cn2-B1 (7.8), which is near neutral: with the p𝐾a set of Table S17, its net charge at pH 7.4 is −0.6. The response also tracked the domain. Design 9a8cdc7a (21% lysine and arginine) and all three fusions carrying it were hits, while none of the four fusions carrying 47d04ba8 (15%) was. Charge does not explain every hit, though. AaHI-B4 (4.2) and the strongest hit, 173c84c2 (4.4), are acidic. Two relatives of AaHI-B4, 91% and 77% identical to it and with the same number of lysines and arginines, were not hits. The correlation also rested on lysine (0.40) rather than arginine (−0.06). The analysis was exploratory, and the designs are not independent, since six of the 11 hits share one backbone family. With one value per family (32 families), 𝜌for the share of lysine and arginine was 0.41 and no longer passed the limit. A repeat at higher salt would test the part played by charge. Ovalbumin is not among the four criteria, and hits against it are flagged. Requiring no hit against ovalbumin as well would leave 15 of the 17 binders (13 of the 15 pairs measured at both labs in Table S16, which does not count the two LqhIII pairs).

S5 In silico scores and measured binding

We tested whether the campaign’s scores could have predicted the binders. The final score tracked how success rates differed between design sets, but within a set it did not pick out the binders (Table S25). Scores and binding fell from the single-toxin to the multi- toxin sets (Figures 4 and 7), and Set 8, the lowest-scoring set against Ts1, AaH I, and LqhIII, had no hits. Within a set and toxin, neither the final score nor the campaign’s rank separated binders from the rest, with one exception: the campaign’s four top-ranked designs of Set 1 (Cn2) are all binders. ipSAE from ESMFold2, the term shown in Table S25, came out highest of the six terms of the final score: within a set and toxin, the ipSAE terms reached an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.71, 0.68, and 0.51 (ESMFold2, ESMFold2-Fast, and Protenix v2) and the scDockQ terms 0.56, 0.60, and 0.50. The mean of the three ipSAE terms, which involves no choice among terms, reached 0.70 (0.53 to 0.83). In a paired bootstrap over designs, ipSAE from ESMFold2 exceeded the final score by 0.11 (95% interval −0.07 to 0.32; 𝑝= 0.26), so 15 binders do not show that any term outperforms the final score. Average precision (AP) led to the same conclusion (Table S25). Within a set and toxin, and in the single-toxin sets, every AP interval includes the chance level. All 60 single-toxin designs scored 0.88 to 0.95 against their toxins, including the 12 made against Ts1, which yielded no binder, and the score did not separate the 11 binders among the 60 from the other 49. The analysis covers the 510 pairs of the 180 campaign designs with their intended toxins for which at least one team returned a usable result. Two of the 17 binders are against LqhIII, which only the Adaptyv Bio team measured, so the first two rows of Table S25 do not count them as binders. Counting them, or leaving out all pairs against LqhIII, changed the AUROC values there by up to 0.02 and the AP values by up to 0.04. The 31 designs added later are left out, as the scores of 28 of them come from a re-run after the campaign.

S5.2 Co-folded positive controls

The three predictors co-folded each antibody control with its toxin as three chains (the variable domains of the heavy and light chains, VH and VL, and the toxin), to test whether the scores against each toxin separate a known complex from decoys (Table S26). Against Cn2, the scores separate known complexes from decoys: 9004G, LR, and RU1 scored above every decoy with each predictor. Against AaH II, 4C1 scored only narrowly above the highest decoy with two of the three. In all 12 cases (four complexes, three predictors), the best seed matched the crystal pose (DockQ 0.82 to 0.95). These four complexes come from three PDB entries that may have been in the predictors’ training data.

Ts1, AaH I, and LqhIII lack such a test, because no complex of serrumab or 9C2 has been solved. Both antibodies scored at most 0.34, close to or below the highest decoy, except 9C2 with Protenix v2, which scored just above it against AaH I (Table S26). In these predictions both partners folded and were in contact. The toxins’ own channel domains scored below the highest decoy with each of the three predictors (Table S26).

S6 Published antibodies and nanobodies against the five toxins

Published antibody fragments and nanobodies against the same toxins remain the standard that designed binders have to beat (Table S27). They bind Cn2 and AaH II with sub-nanomolar reported 𝐾D values, and several protected mice in challenge stud- ies with lethal doses of toxin or venom. Against these two toxins, our tightest binders by 𝐾D at Twist Bioscience were Cn2-B1, with 𝐾D upper limits of 3.1 nM at Adaptyv Bio and 1.7 nM at Twist Bioscience, and AaHII-B1, with 78 nM at Adaptyv Bio and 1.7 nM at Twist Bioscience (Table 1). Against AaH I, AaHI-B1 gave 26 nM at Twist Bioscience and about 6.5 nM at Adaptyv Bio, the mean of one SPR and one BLI replicate (9.7 and 3.2 nM). Our designs have not been tested in functional assays or mice, so the comparison covers affinity only.
