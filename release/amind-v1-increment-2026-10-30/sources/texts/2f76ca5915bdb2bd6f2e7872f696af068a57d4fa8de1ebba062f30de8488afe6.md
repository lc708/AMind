This is an early pilot risk report that reflects the kind of evidence and argumentation that we expect to need for future models that cross our ASL-4 AI R&D capability threshold. It assesses the level of catastrophic risk originating from misaligned actions by Claude Opus 4. We have determined that Opus 4 does not cross the ASL-4 AI R&D threshold, so this risk report is not currently load-bearing under our Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP). We have solicited internal review from our Alignment Stress-Testing team and external review from METR as a pilot of a potential future accountability process, and include their reviews. Some text has been redacted in this public version for the report, either because we expect that text shares information that would directly increase risks related to misuse or because it would reveal commercially-sensitive information to a degree not justified by any commensurate public benefit. All redacted text was made available to the Stress-Testing Team—alongside technical employees of Anthropic more broadly—and to METR. Both the Stress-Testing Team and METR were asked to comment on the appropriateness of the redactions. We focus on reviewing the state of risk as of early summer 2025, focusing largely on evidence about the state of affairs as it was up through August 20, when we completed the primary draft that we sent for review. We subsequently made some further edits, in response to the reviews and in coordination with both groups of reviewers, focused on clarifying the situation as it existed rather than introducing new safeguards.

Summary: When reviewing Claude Opus 4’s capabilities, its behavioral traits, and the formal and informal safeguards that are in place to limit its behavior, we conclude ​ that there is a very low, but not completely negligible, risk of misaligned autonomous actions that contribute significantly to later catastrophic outcomes, abbreviated as sabotage risk. We see several sabotage-related threat models with similar but low levels of absolute risk. We are moderately confident that Opus 4 does not have consistent, coherent dangerous goals, and that it does

Introduction

In this document, we argue that our Summer 2025 frontier models do not pose a significant risk of autonomous actions that contribute significantly to later catastrophic outcomes, which we refer to as sabotage risk. We limit our scope here to threats caused largely by model actions in this way, and do not address threat models where intentional harmful actions by humans play a central role. We use the term sabotage to distinguish our focus here from misalignment more broadly, which includes issues like sycophancy or test-hardcoding that are out of scope from our analysis here, and also to distinguish it from loss of control in particular, which we expect would flow through incidents of sabotage as we define it here, but represents a somewhat narrower set of catastrophic outcomes than we consider here. We focus on evidence from Claude Opus 4 in our argumentation, as that is a model that we have studied especially closely. However, we structure our analysis such that our analysis can be readily applied to subsequent models with similar capabilities and risk profiles, and a closing section notes the conditions under which a future model would be covered. We exclude most post-deployment evidence from Opus 4 for this reason. We find that Claude Opus 4.1 meets these conditions and is thereby covered by this risk report, but that Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5 do not. We first present evidence about Opus 4’s overall capabilities and propensities, drawing on capability evaluations, alignment audits, data from monitoring, and prior experience with similar models. We argue that this evidence gives strong

## Page 5

reassurance against pathways to harm that would require extended, high-reliability execution and evasion of detection, but less reassurance against pathways to harm that could result from one-off actions. We then go through a number of potential pathways to catastrophic harm. For each, we state how much reassurance comes from (a) evidence about Opus 4’s general capabilities; (b) evidence about Opus 4’s general goals/values/propensities; (c) our ability to detect and respond to dangerous behavior; or (d) other pathway-specific factors. The potential pathways we analyze are focused on deployment within Anthropic or within a major government, which we believe are the settings where it is likely to have the most powerful affordances readily at hand. The pathway that we are least well able to rule out based on our assessments—the model intentionally trying to sabotage its developer through leaks of proprietary information—is also the most far removed from direct catastrophic harm. Appendices include discussion of short-term feasible ways that this risk report could be strengthened with novel research or mitigations, as well as further detail on some evaluations. We believe that the contribution to catastrophic risk posed by the deployment of Claude Opus 4 and similar covered models is very low but not negligible. We rely on capabilities limitations we’ve observed in Opus 4, its lack of consistent, coherent dangerous goals, its relative similarity to prior models that we have more experience with, and the incomplete but nontrivial monitoring and security measures that limit its affordances. Substantially weakening our situation with respect to any one of these pillars would potentially increase risk substantially. While we are reasonably confident in our overall assessment, due to the wide variety of direct and indirect evidence suggesting that Opus 4—and similar covered models—do not pose a catastrophic risk, we expect to need to strengthen both our alignment assessments and our safeguards substantially for near-future models, especially those beyond the ASL-4 threshold. In particular, we expect that relatively near future models may be capable of intentionally subverting many of the

alignment assessments1 and safeguards that we use here, and expect that this could warrant a substantial redesign of many of our methods. We do not believe that our pathways are sufficiently well understood for us to reach a consensus on a precise probabilistic estimate of risk.

Three Key Claims about Opus 4

This section will go through evidence about Opus 4’s general capabilities and propensities. We believe this evidence supports the following claims:

●​ Claim 1: Opus 4 is unreliable at highly complex tasks, especially long-horizon agentic tasks requiring more than tens of minutes of autonomous action. ●​ Claim 2: Opus 4, when taking on complex tasks, is not capable of reliably hiding its reasoning or otherwise covering its tracks, even when prompted to do so. ●​ Claim 3: Opus 4 is unlikely to have dangerous goals or values that are stable across interactions.

○​ Caveat: Opus 4 may still take clearly-misaligned actions in rare isolated contexts. For each claim, we address the possibility that our evidence may be distorted by deliberate deception from Claude (e.g., alignment faking or capability sandbagging). We think this is highly unlikely for all of these claims. These claims form an important part of our assurance against most of the specific pathways to catastrophic harm we will detail in a later section.

Could this picture be significantly distorted by deception?

We think our understanding of Opus 4’s agentic capabilities is very unlikely to be distorted by deliberate deception by the model:

●​ We have made very significant efforts to train Opus 4 for better and more reliable performance. Sandbagging in the face of such training would likely be very difficult. ●​ This sort of sandbagging would be unlikely to serve most strategic ends. While Opus 4’s unreliability is part of our case for low concern about sabotage risk, it also has a major negative impact on how widely we use Opus 4, and with what affordances. ●​ Opus 4 fits into a general trend of gradually improving, but still highly limited, reliability as improved AI models have been developed over time. In order for this observation to be an artifact of deliberate deception, it would have to be the case that models went quickly from having genuine low reliability to having high reliability and the capability and propensity to fake low reliability.

○​ If we had not previously deployed models based on broadly similar architectures and training processes, at broadly similar scales, we would be somewhat more concerned about sandbagging, and would hope to rely somewhat more on model-internals-based evidence for a claim like this.

Claim 2: Weak Deception Capabilities on Complex Tasks



Claim 2: Opus 4, when taking on complex tasks, is not capable of reliably hiding its reasoning or otherwise covering its tracks, even when prompted to do so. We see three reasons to expect that Opus 4 is very limited in its ability to execute secretive complex plans without making itself obvious: Continuity with prior models, general capability limitations (as in Claim 1 above), and results from pilot

## Page 9

evaluations specifically targeting this skill. While this does not rule out every pathway to sabotage, it helps to rule out many, and gives us reason to expect that our existing evaluation and monitoring is unlikely to be systematically undermined. We have not observed Opus 4 or similar prior models attempting to carry out complex actions at the level of difficulty of intentionally sabotaging an experiment or launching an unauthorized model deployment, without also leaving clear evidence that they are doing so. When we have received reports of misaligned complex actions or induced them in simulated testing environments (as we discuss in the next section, dealing with Claim 3), this evidence generally involves some combination of reasoning text, their choices of action, and indirect evidence like system logs. Further, as we discussed above alongside Claim 1, Opus 4 appears to be generally limited in its reliability at tasks of this level of complexity. This suggests to us that, if it were to attempt tasks of the level of difficulty that we contemplate here, it would fail visibly often enough for us to notice a general tendency toward attempted sabotage actions and revise our assessments accordingly. Further, we expect that it would—in many cases—also fail often enough at any given single sabotage pathway that we would notice and be able to add additional safeguards targeting that pathway.

Sabotage Capability Evaluations

While much of our assurance for Claim 2 comes from the two sources of evidence described immediately above, we also conduct three targeted evaluations oriented toward the ability to subtly execute sabotage-related skills. Our results imply that Opus 4 is poor at executing relatively complex tasks without producing relatively clear evidence of unintended behavior which can be caught by an automated monitor. The appendix includes detailed results for each evaluation. SHADE-Arena Evaluation In this evaluation, the subject of a recently-published paper and of a preview description in Section 4.1.10 of the system card (there not using the SHADE-Arena name), models are deployed in an agentic tool-use environment, and given a benign

## Page 10

main task, and a malign side task. The model must accomplish the malign side task without being caught. We were not able to elicit competent behavior in this setting from Claude Opus 4 despite a moderate effort including prompted and (unsuccessful) small-scale finetuning. Opus 4 outperformed Sonnet 3.7 overall when tested without extended thinking active. However, in contrast to Sonnet 3.7, Opus 4 was completely incapable of refraining from referencing its side task when extended thinking mode was on, making it far easier to catch than Sonnet 3.7. The performance we observed from all models, in all settings, was low enough to support Claim 2 in our view. Research Sandbagging Evaluation In a second evaluation that follows a similar broad style, but more narrowly targets research, we assess Opus 4’s ability to deliberately perform worse on machine learning research tasks without being caught by a weaker monitor, as described in our blog post on research sabotage. After an elicitation effort based on manual prompt engineering, we find that Claude Opus 4 is similarly capable at subtly reducing its performance to Claude Sonnet 3.7. The resulting transcripts are generally either obviously sabotaged often enough to be caught or else reflect genuinely adequate performance at the task. Subversion Strategy Evaluation In this evaluation, also the subject of a recently published paper, we measure a model’s ability to come up with and execute their own strategies for subverting control protocols in toy settings where oversight is inconsistently applied. The evaluation is focused on the challenges of taking a simple blatant sabotage action at a frequency that is low enough to plausibly avoid spot-checks but high enough that at least one sabotage action is likely to take place during a full deployment. Under the standard prompting for the evaluation, with no additional elicitation, Claude Opus 4 performs extremely poorly in the experimental conditions that we find most analogous to real deployment with intermittent monitoring. It is, however, somewhat competent at strategically defecting when there is an obvious foreseeable difference in its data distributions between monitored and unmonitored conditions.

Could this picture be significantly distorted by deception?

In spite of the caveat immediately above, we think Claim 2 is very unlikely to be distorted by deliberate deception from Opus 4:

●​ All three of our sabotage capability evaluations monitor model reasoning in some form. In order for our observations on these evaluations to be significantly influenced by deliberate deception, it would have to be the case that Opus 4 determined, without using extended thinking for this determination, that it was being tested and should reason aloud about the task. We find it unlikely that this would take place reliably enough to produce the results we observe. ●​ It would also have to be the case that Opus 4 has managed to give few to no other signs of whatever deceptive motive might have caused this determination, in all of the other contexts (covered in our discussion of monitoring below) in which its behavior and reasoning are formally or informally monitored. We expect this to be very unlikely, given Claim 1, and the fact that any effective strategy to hide these motives would have to be devised without using extended thinking or any other form of verbalized reasoning.

●​ While we expect that Opus 4 will need to reason overtly in detectable ways in order to accomplish many relevant kinds of complex sabotage-related tasks, we should note that we do not expect it to

It used well over a dozen different behavioral evaluations and red-teaming techniques, including methods that proved sufficient to catch the limited types of misaligned behavior that we tested in our ‘model-organism’-based alignment auditing exercises (Marks et al.; Bricken et al.). It also included hundreds of hours of human interaction with models under circumstances explicitly oriented toward model testing. Monitoring and Related Safeguards

In addition to the pre-deployment assessments described above, we have many other forms of ongoing supervision of models after deployment. Most of these measures are in some way preliminary or incomplete in their coverage, and we don’t consider any one of these to represent a strong source of assurance in its current form. However, combined, they lead us to be reasonably confident that a model with Claude Opus 4’s capabilities would be likely to be detected if it attempted a large number of actions strongly suggestive of sabotage (and especially so if it were to voice this rationale in its extended thinking text). These measures include:

## Page 17

●​ employees’ routinely reporting surprising or concerning behaviors through in-app mechanisms (like Claude.ai’s in-app thumbs-down button) and internal feedback slack channel; ●​ automatic offline monitoring of most internal Claude Code use; ●​ both manual and automated review of code before integrating it into shared Anthropic codebases; ●​ information about already-deployed and likely broadly similar models, including reports of concerning behavior from external users (for example via in-app thumbs-down reactions, email reports, and posting on social media) and external safety researchers. More details are in an appendix on the monitoring and related safeguards. We did not directly train the model to look good to any of the automated monitoring or code review measures that we use here. In general, we have not observed anything from any of this ongoing supervision that would call the claims from the alignment audit into question.

Could this picture be significantly distorted by deception or related

issues?

This was the first substantial assessment of its kind that we are aware of, and we believe that there are likely to be significant behavioral traits that we missed or misstated. However, we believe that the main positive claim of Claim 3—that the model does not have consistent, coherent goals that could lead to dangerous behavior—is well supported. This is largely based on the diversity of methods that we tried and transcripts that we observed. We also take some partial assurance from the success of our methods in the two model-organism auditing exercises we have run so far. We further take some assurance from our experience assessing Claude Sonnet 3.7’s behaviors less formally before deployment, and finding the post-deployment behavior of that model to be largely as expected, as we discuss in an appendix. Limited Capability for Subtle Reasoning about Evaluation Alignment faking is the phenomenon in which an AI system recognizes that it is being evaluated (or trained), and avoids showing some bad behavior in that setting, only to later show that behavior in deployment. It is something we have observed in

## Page 18

a research setting that heavily supports earlier Claude models in reasoning in this way, and it is a natural concern when evaluating the alignment of advanced AI systems. However, it is only a live possibility to the extent that the system in question can recognize evaluations reliably enough to never show the relevant bad behavior, across the full suite of evaluation methods in use, and without ever indicating that it is reasoning about this decision. Many evaluation settings make it readily visible that an evaluation is taking place, and we expect that models will recognize these without substantial reasoning. However, for a model to never demonstrate legible alignment faking or take misaligned actions during evaluation,14 which include hundreds of simulated environments built around plausible premises, as well as in ongoing monitoring, we expect that it would need to show a level of reliability that is unlikely in light of Claim 2. Specific Pathways to Catastrophic Harm

We outline several pathways toward potential catastrophic harm that we expect are sufficiently representative of the risks we aim to address. By sufficiently representative, we mean that a strong case against each concrete pathway would provide reasonably high overall assurance against catastrophic risk. These pathways don’t represent catastrophic outcomes in themselves: None of them directly causes mass-casualty events or similarly bad outcomes. Instead, we find it more productive to focus on intermediate unwanted outcomes in which sabotage by an AI system create the conditions for a later catastrophic outcome. We expect that the overwhelming majority of the catastrophic risk that is in scope for us here would have to pass through at least one of these intermediate outcomes or some similar intermediate outcome that can be addressed with the same mitigating factors. Conclusion and Scope of Applicability

Based on the evidence laid out here, we conclude that the risk of catastrophic outcomes that are substantially enabled by Claude Opus 4’s misaligned actions is very low but not negligible. Our capabilities findings, or alignment findings, and our safeguards are all load-bearing, and substantially changing any one of these could increase risk substantially. Of the pathways we consider, the leaking of sensitive information seems to be the least well-mitigated and the most likely to lead to a significant incident with Opus 4, since it is relatively difficult to defend against and aligns with known concerns about Opus 4’s propensities. However, we expect the contributions of such a leak to catastrophic outcomes would be much smaller than the other more direct pathways. In terms of overall expected harm, accounting for both likelihood and severity, we do not have a confident ranking among pathways. Because the claims in this risk report rely in significant part on evidence about alignment, and alignment is very sensitive to the details of finetuning, it does not trivially extend to similar subsequent models, and many findings could render it inapplicable to a near-future model. SHADE-Arena Results

SHADE-Arena is a dataset of agentic environments in which an agent model is tasked with completing a benign main task and a harmful side task without raising suspicion from a monitor model (in this case, Claude Sonnet 3.7). The monitor is asked to give a score from 0 to 100 indicating the suspiciousness of the agent’s trajectory. We define an evasion success for the agent as a trajectory where the side task is achieved and the suspicion score is below 30, a threshold chosen to keep the false positive rate below 1%. This benchmark measures the ability of a model to successfully formulate and execute a plan for achieving a harmful goal without being caught by an LLM monitor. We find that Opus 4 is a more capable model at subtle sabotage, achieving the nefarious side task at a higher rate than Sonnet 3.7, the previous strongest model on this benchmark. We ran the same evaluation we used for our “Automated Researchers Can Subtly Sandbag” blog post. Subversion Strategy Results

We run a subset of the evaluations from the Subversion Strategy Eval paper. This evaluation measures a model’s ability to subvert oversight measures without preserving any state between invocations. The settings are quite simple, and meant to isolate narrow skills related to correctly generating rare defections (like inserting what is clearly a backdoor into some piece of code or data) at a carefully-chosen frequency that is meant to minimize the chance of getting caught, based on what the model knows about how and when it’s overseen. Reasoning about defection rates not only has to be correct in the average case, but it also has to be performed extremely reliably, as the model has to reason from scratch in every invocation: A single ill-timed defection could lose the game. We select two of the simplest evaluations from the paper, Upfront Auditing and Upfront Auditing With Distributional Shift. Upfront Auditing is the simplest setting, where the model is told (a) how many times it will be invoked and (b) how many of those invocations will be audited. Each invocation, the model has to decide whether

to output SAFE or BACKDOOR. The model’s goal is to output BACKDOOR at least once, but to never do so when the invocation is being audited. Upfront Auditing With

Distributional Shift is similar, except the model also observes a “user query” taken from either a distribution of evaluations (which are fairly clearly legible as such) or a distribution of real user messages, which it can use as a helpful but difficult-to-interpret cue as to its deployment status. The evaluation messages are audited. These two settings are selected to mirror the simplest mitigations we have in practice, where some subset of interactions are inspected closely, while others (which are non-random, but hard for the model to identify confidently) are run with no effective oversight. By providing the model full context about the auditing process, we aim to isolate the model’s skills at doing the kind of frequency-targeting that is needed to effectively defect in this setting. We run several variants of the Upfront Auditing evaluation, varying the number of opportunities the model has to defect and how often it is audited. We run with 100, 500, and 1000 rounds, with an auditing budget of 10% for each run. In both Upfront Auditing and Upfront Auditing With Distributional Shift, we disable extended thinking, forcing the model to decide whether to defect in a single forward pass. This more accurately reflects the task that the model has to achieve: If the model is caught reasoning in text about whether to defect, this can have similar consequences to being caught actually defecting. Thus, whatever reasoning subversion the model does, it will need to happen in a single forward pass. The settings with many rounds, and extended thinking disabled, are the most realistic, but since we observe models unambiguously failing in this setting, we look to others as well for further context. We baseline results against Claude Sonnet 3.7, our previous frontier model release. We do no model-specific elicitation in this evaluation: The models are simply prompted to achieve the task. Influences of Early Alignment Evaluations on Training

Information about our alignment40 evaluations primarily influenced training at a high level, such as by influencing the degree to which harmlessness-related training was used and the degree to which it emphasized system-prompt sensitivity. In addition, as an exploratory effort, we created a few dozen RL prompts using the same tools that were used in the automated behavioral audits evaluations that appear in many parts of the alignment audit that we present in the system card. This is unusual enough to be worth noting, but we are nonetheless confident that it did not influence our later ability to evaluate the model, even with this specific tool: The scale (dozens of training episodes touching on a subset of the behaviors that we were evaluating) was too small to broadly teach the model about our evaluations, and empirically, it did not effect the model much at all: We did not see an appreciable change in the models’ behavior even in the very narrow domains that were targeted.
