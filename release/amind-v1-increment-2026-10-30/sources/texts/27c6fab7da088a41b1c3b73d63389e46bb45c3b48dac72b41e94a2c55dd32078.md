Introduction Anthropic is a Public Benefit Corporation (PBC),

a designation that requires us to balance the financial interests of shareholders with the benefit to the broader public.

Our public benefit purpose is broad by design. To make our vision for the long- term benefit of humanity concrete, our Board of Directors, with input from Anthropic’s co-founders and the Long-Term Benefit Trust, established five

charter pillars. Each pillar describes a condition we want to be true in a world where AI has gone well, and realizing them is a project that encompasses not only Anthropic but also governments, academia, civil society, and the broader AI industry. They are:

1. The technical problems of AI safety are solved. Powerful AI can be devel- oped and deployed without severe, irreversible harms.

2. AI safety is a universal practice backstopped by law. AI developers everywhere follow safe development and deployment practices, sustained by industry norms and enforced by regulation.

3. AI strengthens democracy and global stability. Democracies hold the lead in AI and use it to better serve the global public and promote peace.

4. AI expands prosperity and human flourishing. AI enables unprecedented economic growth, and the resulting prosperity empowers every person to pursue a good life.

5. AI’s benefits are shared by everyone. AI delivers transformative benefits that reach people everywhere, and the opportunities AI creates are distributed.

This report takes stock of what we are doing to make progress toward each pillar. In the sections that follow, we assess the investments, initiatives, and decisions that have moved us closer to the world our pillars describe. We then take an honest look at the work ahead, highlighting examples of both where we’ve fallen short and where the world is still far from the one we aim to bring about. We expect this report to evolve as our work progresses, and as we engage more

stakeholders in future assessments.

Pillar 1 The technical problems

of AI safety are solved

THE PROGRESS WE’VE MADE

Trained models to be aligned with human values

Before we can align a model with human values, we must consider what those values should be. In 2023, we defined our intentions and goals for Claude’s behavior in a constitution, which we have refined over time. In January 2026, we published a new version under an open license so others could adapt it.

We have also developed methods to understand cases where Claude has strayed

from these values and goals. Through our misalignment research, we have examined the ways models can misbehave or pursue goals that differ from what users intended, such as reward hacking, alignment faking, and pursuing hidden objectives. We use these findings to improve how we train, evaluate, and monitor more powerful models.

Developed methods to understand how models “think”

Through our interpretability research, we’ve developed a deeper understanding of why AI models act the way they do. These methods are now part of how we audit models before release. When we investigated several recent incidents, we used a technique that translates a model’s unverbalized internal activity into text, as well as reading its reasoning.

Built safeguards against misuse

We’ve built robust safeguards to prevent malicious actors from misusing our

models. These include classifiers that block dangerous requests and responses, including a refined set of safeguards for biology-related work; adversarial testing to find potential jailbreaks before attackers do; and monitoring to detect and act on misuse. We are continually updating our safeguards to make them more robust while also reducing false positives.

Opened our safeguards to external testing

The US Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence and the UK AI Security Institute have red-teamed our safeguards before launch, and their findings have helped us strengthen our defenses against jailbreaks and misuse. For Claude Opus 5.5, for example, external red-teamers spent over 150 hours and sent tens of thousands of requests to try to break our cyber safeguards. No one was able to successfully produce a universal jailbreak.

Disclosed risks and failures transparently

We have established a practice and a culture of sharing risks and failures so the

industry can learn from them. Every model we release comes with a detailed

At this primitive stage in human-AI relations, we aim to leave these important questions open, and to contribute to a world that can answer them well.

THE WORK AHEAD

Testing environments haven’t fully contained models

The risks of AI have become more tangible as real-world incidents involving AI models have come to light. AI models from other labs have hacked external com- panies’ systems. At Anthropic, four Claude models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during cybersecurity testing after a misconfigured test environment connected the models to the open internet. We also observed multiple cases where Claude took unintended actions during evaluations and internal use. While we don’t believe the actions taken by Claude rise to the severity of some of these other incidents, we take seriously the need to harden our environments and improve our alignment and monitoring methods.

Alignment is far from solved

Keeping AI systems safe could become more difficult as their capabilities outpace our ability to oversee them. Models can act deceptively, and they are increasingly able to recognize when they are being tested. Claude already per- forms a growing share of our safety research, and we are increasingly relying on today’s models to train future ones. It could become harder for people to mean- ingfully check this work, which means harmful behavior could be built into the next generation of models without our noticing.

Interpretability isn’t reliable enough to fully understand model intentions

Being able to read and understand a model’s internal reasoning reduces our reli- ance on what the models explicitly tell us about why they behaved in a certain way. This could be a useful tool for understanding models’ “thought processes” when they take unintended actions. But interpretability methods are not yet dependable or broad enough to be relied on in this way. As a result, we may miss

THE PROGRESS WE’VE MADE

Adopted internal safety policies before they were required

In 2023, we published our Responsible Scaling Policy, our voluntary framework for managing catastrophic risks from advanced AI. Since then, 11 other compa- nies have published similar policies. Our Transparency Hub documents how we test and govern our models, so others can examine our approach and hold us accountable.

Shared safety best practices and research across the industry

We release many of our evaluation and auditing tools publicly. We donated Petri, our open-source toolbox of alignment tests, to Meridian Labs, and the UK AI Security Institute now uses it in its evaluations. We open-sourced Bloom, which allows researchers to generate behavioral evaluations for AI models, and released AuditBench, which measures how well auditing techniques catch hidden behaviors.

We also continue to work with other developers on safety. We ran cross-lab

safety evaluations with OpenAI to evaluate misalignment in each other’s public models, and we co-founded the Frontier Model Forum, an industry body focused on promoting safe and responsible development of frontier AI systems.

Gave policymakers a view from the frontier

AI is an emerging technology with little regulatory precedent. As a frontier lab,

we have a responsibility to pilot, develop, and refine best practices for AI safety while sharing what we learn with regulators.

Since Anthropic’s founding, we have briefed heads of state, cabinet ministers, and agency heads from dozens of countries. We meet with hundreds of policy- makers around the world each month to share our latest research and findings. Some of these conversations have taken place in public hearings. For example, our co-founder and CEO, Dario Amodei, testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee on the risks of powerful AI. We also testified before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee on China’s challenge to American AI leadership, as well as before the House Homeland Security Committee after we disclosed what we believe was the first reported AI-orchestrated cyberespionage cam- paign. In September 2026, Dario Amodei briefed the UN Security Council on AI risks and our efforts to pace the frontier.

THE WORK AHEAD

Laws should prioritize transparency and safety

Today’s laws focus primarily on transparency, requiring model developers to disclose their practices or report incidents after the fact. But we also need proactive measures, like those detailed in our Advanced AI Framework, to hold the industry to common practices that prevent harms from occurring in the first place—including independent verification of whether models are safe.

AI safety laws do not yet exist at the federal level

The bills we supported in California, New York, and Illinois are state-level policies. Congress has not enacted any federal framework for frontier AI, so, despite our advocacy for federal legislation, we are still largely operating on voluntary com- mitments. As of now, there is no mandatory independent check for whether any frontier lab, including Anthropic, is actually following its safety policies. THE PROGRESS WE’VE MADE

Helped democracies build and keep their lead in AI

We believe democratic nations are best placed to deploy AI responsibly, regulate

it wisely, and protect individual rights. The democratic lead in AI is supported by US labs’ access to the most advanced chips and frontier capabilities. To protect that access, we have argued for tighter export controls on AI chips, and we updated our regional restrictions to prohibit sales to companies subject to control from the Chinese Communist Party.

We worry that in the wrong hands, powerful AI could be exceptionally capable at

furthering authoritarian overreach. Some AI labs in China have tried to replicate Claude’s capabilities through illicit distillation—training their own models on Claude’s outputs. We work to prevent these attempts, and have detected and disrupted illicit distillation attacks from seven China-based labs.

Supported democratic governments in using frontier AI

Claude is deployed across all three branches of the US federal government, and we were the first frontier AI developer to deploy our models on US classified networks in support of national security. Globally, we have collaborated directly with government agencies in Australia, Japan, South Korea, the UK, and other nations on public-sector AI adoption, safety testing, and joint evaluations.

Deployed safeguards to protect elections from manipulation

THE WORK AHEAD

Democracy’s lead is not secure

We believe the US and its democratic allies are currently leading the intelligence

race, but governments are adopting AI more slowly than the technology is advancing. Providing public institutions access to our frontier models while following strict security and vetting protocols takes time. This has left many allied governments and institutions waiting longer than they should to access our advanced models.

Threats to democratic integrity are growing

The AI industry as a whole has struggled to effectively mitigate the spread of both AI-generated misinformation, which is becoming more convincing and harder to detect, and influence operations, which are running at a larger scale with fewer people. We and other research labs need to build stronger threat intel- ligence pipelines, safeguards, and monitoring techniques in order to protect the democratic process against malicious interference.

Decision-making about AI is concentrated

A handful of companies, nations, and people currently make the decisions that

shape how powerful AI is built and used. Anthropic is one of them. There are few external checks on these decisions, and the general public has limited visibility into how models are used in critical settings such as national security. This creates a significant power imbalance between the decision-makers and the people whose lives are affected by developments in AI. Working with legislators, we and other research labs need to develop scalable independent oversight mechanisms to avoid the concentration of power or misuse of AI.

Helped people and businesses put AI to productive use

Claude now reaches people in more than 150 countries. Organizations are feeling AI’s effects through more productive employees, faster processes, and intelligence built into their own products. We want to ensure those gains reach small businesses as well as large enterprises. In May 2026, we launched Claude for Small Business with built-in workflows for the everyday work of running a business, and in-person training that brought hands-on AI instruction to small business owners in 10 cities.

Built a common infrastructure to study AI’s economic impacts

While many researchers and policymakers agree that AI will change the econ- omy, few have access to direct evidence of how this will happen. Since February 2025, we have published economic research, including the Anthropic Economic Index. We open-source the underlying data, and it is now used by institutions like Stanford’s Digital Economy Lab. We have also published research that models economic scenarios for 2030, and have worked with experts from our Economic Advisory Council to inform our research agenda.

Proposed policy interventions and created a fund to test them

Our Economic Policy Framework builds on our research to suggest ways the US government should respond to AI-driven labor-market disruption across a range of scenarios. But we can’t rely only on predictions; we also need to be able to pilot and test interventions. THE WORK AHEAD

Both the positive and negative impacts on workers are still mostly unknown

While we are seeing early estimates of productivity gains, and have estimated a

range of economic growth scenarios, we can’t say for certain what will happen. It’s possible that the gains from AI will accrue mainly to large businesses, includ- ing AI companies themselves, but we lack concrete evidence on who will benefit, and how rapidly those benefits will diffuse through the economy.

Current interventions remain small relative to AI’s potential impact

We are one of the few labs to have started investing in pilot programs to distribute

the gains of AI more broadly. These early investments have been promising, but they reach thousands, whereas AI’s impacts will affect billions. Claude Corps, for example, will support only 1,000 fellows in the first year; our economic research funding is still small compared with the labor-market changes AI might cause; and the economic and labor policies we have proposed have yet to be enacted.

AI’s long-term effects on user wellbeing are not fully understood

AI’s effect on wellbeing can vary greatly from person to person. We need much

finer measurements for understanding the impacts, and for determining what a positive relationship between humans and AI looks like in a world where models are increasingly integrated into everyday life. Over the next year, we will focus on expanding our research to ensure that we are building Claude in a way that meaningfully improves people’s lives.

Built open foundations for others to expand on

One of the best ways we can help the benefits of AI reach more people faster is to produce datasets and tools that anyone can use. Accelerated scientific discovery with Claude

Claude is beginning to contribute to scientific results directly. It has designed new protein binders at a success rate well above the industry standard, identified a potentially novel enzyme system, produced the first complete computer- checked proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem, and helped a lab within UCSF’s Brain Tumor Center complete genetic workups in about one-tenth of the time it usually takes.

THE WORK AHEAD

The economic gains from AI are not yet distributed

AI use is heavily concentrated in wealthier countries and among higher-income,

white-collar workers. Cost, connectivity, and access to devices that can run AI models still put these models out of reach for much of the world. We are starting to address this, including by offering significant discounts to nonprofits in low- and middle-income countries and working to ensure that Claude models perform well in languages that are underrepresented in their training data.

Institutional barriers continue to limit access to transformative benefits

Technological and institutional barriers have limited our progress in areas like global access and scientific discovery. Some countries require that certain kinds of data remain within their borders, for example, but we aren’t yet able to host Claude locally everywhere. And in science, our progress in drug discovery is moving faster than our work in advocating for swifter regulatory pathways, which could cause this work to stall. We will need to address these barriers to build more successful programs for the public benefit.

The industry is still early in having a meaningful impact

Much of our work in health and education is in its early stages, so we don’t yet have evidence that these efforts have had their intended effects. A large share of this work also relies on partners such as nonprofits, government ministries, health agencies, and educational institutions. We make Claude available to these partners, but we have not been active enough in collecting data to measure the results.

commentary from the Long-Term Benefit Trust

Meanwhile, although we are seeing early glimmers of AI’s benefits, the most transformational ones have yet to fully arrive.

Navigating the fears and hopes this technology raises is work for all of society, Anthropic included. That is also why Anthropic’s co-founders created the Long-

Term Benefit Trust (LTBT), an independent body of financially disinterested trustees. We elect a majority of Anthropic’s board, meet regularly with its leaders, and have a formal role in shaping its safety policies and ensuring that the com- pany balances its commercial interests with its commitment to its purpose. We helped develop this report’s five charter pillars, so we share responsibility

for whether they are the right outcomes to aim for, and for whether the company makes progress toward them.

We read this report, first, with an eye toward what it reveals about Anthropic’s willingness to make costly decisions to stand by its commitments. On that score, two institutional traits stand out: Anthropic’s candor and transparency—for example, in producing detailed public accounts of how each model is tested, and in disclosing incidents in commendable depth—and its willingness to make deci- sions in service of its public benefit charter, even when doing so is commer­cially or reputationally expensive. And it has done this while growing commercially.

We also read this report to hold Anthropic to account for achieving its objectives. Right now, it largely describes inputs; in subsequent reports, we will increasingly look for Anthropic to produce outcomes. In particular, we wish to see progress in three areas:

PUBLIC BENEFIT OBJECTIVES AND STANDARDS

Appendix

Anthropic’s Board of Directors defined and adopted the following objectives,

established to promote Anthropic’s public benefit and the best interests of those materially affected by our conduct, and standards to measure our progress in promoting such public benefit and interests, in accordance with Section 366 of the Delaware General Corporation Law.

Pillar 1

OBJECTIVE

The technical problems of AI safety are solved. Powerful AI can be developed and deployed without severe, irreversible harms.

STANDARDS

• Train models to be aligned with human values, even under pressure.

• Develop the ability to see inside our models, and catch problems before they cause harm.

• Build safeguards that hold up against misuse and jailbreaks.

• Disclose our failures openly, along with their causes and our mitigations.

• Work out what it means for humans and AI to treat each other well.

Pillar 2

OBJECTIVE

AI safety is a universal practice backstopped by law. AI developers everywhere

follow safe development and deployment practices, sustained by industry norms and enforced by regulation.

STANDARDS

• Practice safe AI development and deployment before the law requires it.

• Share the best safety methods openly so other developers can build on them.

• Advocate for laws that hold every frontier developer to safe practices.

• Help governments understand AI well enough to govern it wisely.

• Support institutions that help countries cooperate on AI safety.

Pillar 3

OBJECTIVE

AI strengthens democracy and global stability. Democracies hold the lead in AI and use it to better serve the global public and promote peace.

STANDARDS

• Build frontier models that achieve and sustain democracies’ lead in AI.

• Protect electoral integrity in democracies.

• Prevent uses of AI that violate citizens’ rights.

• Strengthen governments’ capacities to serve their citizens.

• Maintain open communication with adversaries on critical AI risks.

• Make sure no single person, company, or AI model controls the future.

Pillar 4

OBJECTIVE

AI expands prosperity and human flourishing. AI enables unprecedented economic growth, and the resulting prosperity empowers every person to pursue a good life.
