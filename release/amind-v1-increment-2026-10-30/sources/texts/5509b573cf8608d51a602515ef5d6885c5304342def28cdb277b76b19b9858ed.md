Preclinical Characterization of a Bispecific Bridging Antibody for the Treatment of LAMA2-Related Congenital Muscular Dystrophy

Cody Tranbarger1,* and Mithril Medicine†

1Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA. †Members are listed in the Acknowledgments. *Correspondence: ctranbarger@anthropic.com

Abstract

LAMA2-related congenital muscular dystrophy (LAMA2-CMD) is a devastating pediatric disease caused by the loss of laminin-α2 for which there is no disease-modifying treatment. Laminin-α2 is the α chain of laminin-211, the heterotrimer that assembles the basement membrane around each muscle fiber and anchors it to the sarcolemma. Muscle lacking laminin-α2 overexpresses a related laminin, laminin-α4, but the protein fails to self-polymerize and binds α-dystroglycan only weakly, and thus fails to compensate for the missing laminin-α2. We hypothesized that a bispecific antibody bridging laminin-α4 to β-dystroglycan (β-DG), the transmembrane subunit of dystroglycan, would restore basement membrane anchorage and sarcolemma stability. We engineered such bispecific bridging antibodies in several formats and studied two in depth, one bivalent and one tetravalent. Both bound their targets with nanomolar affinity and bridged dystroglycan- overexpressing cells to a recombinant fragment of laminin-α4. In a pilot study in laminin-α2-deficient dyW mice, the bivalent antibody was recovered from muscle days after last dose, at concentrations exceeding those required for bridging in vitro. Meanwhile, mice treated with the tetravalent construct developed about twice the peak force and grip strength of vehicle-treated mice. These data identify antibody design as a major determinant of bridging efficiency, suggest that reaching translationally relevant antibody exposures in muscle is feasible, and provide proof of principle for the therapeutic approach, supporting the continued progression of Anthropic’s bispecific bridging antibody programs in LAMA2-CMD and α-dystroglycanopathy.

Children with LAMA2-related congenital muscular dystrophy (LAMA2-CMD), caused by the loss of laminin-α2 (ref. 1), are hypotonic and weak from birth or early infancy.⁠2 In one cohort of 42 children with complete deficiency, a single child walked independently, about half needed nocturnal ventilation and one in six died before adulthood.⁠3 Care remains supportive, and no disease-modifying treatment is available.⁠4–6 Laminin-α2 is the α chain of laminin-211 (α2β1γ1),⁠7 the heterotrimer that assembles the basement membrane around each muscle fiber and anchors it to the sarcolemma. Laminin-211 polymerizes through the laminin N-terminal (LN) domains at the tips of its short arms, and each node of the polymer joins one α, one β and one γ LN domain from neighboring molecules.⁠8 At the opposite end, laminin-211 binds integrin α7β1 and, through the laminin globular (LG) domains of its α chain, α-dystroglycan (α-DG), whose transmem­ brane partner β-dystroglycan (β-DG) binds dystrophin.⁠9–11 Loss of laminin-α2 therefore removes both polymerization and anchor­ age, leaving a disrupted basement membrane around fibers that degenerate from the first months of life.⁠12,13

Polymerization and anchorage can nevertheless be supplied to laminin-411 in trans by two engineered linker proteins, αLNNd, which gives laminin-411 a polymerizing arm, and mini-agrin, which links laminin to α-DG.15,21,22 Each linker fits one adeno- associated virus (AAV) vector,⁠23,24 whereas the LAMA2 coding sequence is about twice the packaging capacity of these vectors.⁠25 In dyW mice, a single intravenous injection of the two vectors on postnatal day 1 brought weight gain, gait speed and muscle mass relative to body mass close to normal at 8 weeks (ref. 24). Injection at 3 weeks of age, at half the dose per kilogram, was less effective but still conferred substantial benefit.⁠24 When the linkers were expressed from transgenes in dyW mice, mini-agrin alone raised median survival from 15.5 to 50 weeks, and the two linkers together extended it to 81 weeks (ref. 15). These experi­ ments showed that the laminin remaining in deficient muscle is a usable scaffold, and that restoring anchorage alone more than tripled survival in this model. Natural history

A bispecific bridging antibody designed to reconnect laminin to the sarcolemma has already been tested in the α-dystrogly­ canopathies, a related group of disorders in which α-DG lacks the glycan that binds laminin-211 (ref. 29). This antibody carried two binding sites each for the last two LG domains (LG4-5) of laminin-α2 and for the ectodomain of β-DG, and it improved grip strength and treadmill running in adult Largemyd-3J mice, a model of these disorders.⁠30 In LAMA2-CMD, the missing con­ nection between the remaining laminin and the sarcolemma is likewise extracellular and in principle accessible to a circulating protein. We therefore hypothesized that a bispecific bridging antibody targeting β-DG and LG4-5 of laminin-α4 would bridge laminin-411 to the sarcolemma and restore anchorage. We kept β-DG as the anchor because it appears to persist in laminin- α2-deficient muscle of mice31 and of two patients with residual laminin-α2 (ref. 32), and because the same anchor could then serve LAMA2-CMD and the α-dystroglycanopathies.

Here, we set out the rationale for a bispecific bridging antibody in LAMA2-CMD and test the hypothesis with two tool molecules built on published variable regions. We show that both antibod­ ies bridge dystroglycan-overexpressing cells to a recombinant fragment of laminin-α4 in vitro, which we take as a proxy for the anchorage they are meant to restore. We also report proof of principle for the therapeutic approach with one of the two antibodies in dyW mice. Finally, we describe how we now work with Claude, a large language model, on this program.

The early-onset form is best documented in 42 London children classed as completely deficient in laminin-α2 (ref. 3; Fig. 1B, C). Weakness and hypotonia are present at or soon after birth.⁠2,3 Most children learn to sit, at about one year, but only 1 of the 42 walked independently. Background and rationale

Genetics, pathophysiology and natural history

Genetics and disease phenotype

Nerve, brain and heart are also involved but seldom dominate the clinical picture (Fig. 1B). White-matter T2 hyperintensity appears after about six months in nearly all patients and is com­ patible with normal cognition.⁠52,53 About one fifth of patients have a cortical malformation,⁠2,41 and 10 to 36% develop epilepsy.⁠3,54 Motor nerve conduction is often slowed,⁠3 but neuropathy rarely manifests clinically and is thought to contribute far less to weakness in patients than in Lama2-mutant mice.⁠5,55,56 Cardiac findings are usually subclinical.⁠2,3 Skeletal muscle therefore limits function and survival in childhood.

Laminin-211 and the muscle basement membrane

Assembly of the basement membrane

Merosin, whose heavy chain is now called laminin-α2, was first described in 1988 as a basement membrane protein of striated muscle, Schwann cells and trophoblast.⁠33 Its absence from muscle of 13 patients with congenital muscular dystrophy (CMD) was reported in 1994 (ref. 34), and a year later the first mutations in LAMA2, which encodes laminin-α2, were identi­ fied.⁠1 LAMA2-related muscular dystrophies are autosomal reces­ sive and span a clinical spectrum from early-onset “classical” LAMA2-CMD, formerly called merosin-deficient CMD (MDC1A), to a limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD R23) with ambulation preserved for decades.⁠5,35

The sarcolemma of each muscle fiber is coated by a basement membrane whose outer face merges with the endomysial colla­ gen that neighboring fibers share (Fig. 2A).⁠57 Contractile force is transmitted to the endomysium laterally, across the sarcolemma and the basement membrane, as well as longitudinally at the ends of the fiber.⁠57 Lateral transmission may account for the severity of the disease that follows loss of a single basement membrane protein, laminin-α2. Consistent with this view, tetanic stress of muscles from dy/dy mice was depressed under uniaxial but not under biaxial load, which suggested that laminin transmits force between adjacent fibers.⁠58 The basement membrane is therefore part of a load-bearing composite, although its share of the load has not been measured.

Disease severity correlates with the class of LAMA2 variant. Of 309 distinct disease-associated variants in the international LAMA2 database, 54% were truncating (nonsense or frameshift) variants, predicted to abolish the protein, and 14% were missense variants or small in-frame deletions, predicted to spare most of it (Fig. 1A).⁠36 The remaining splice-site and copy-number variants can abolish laminin-α2 or leave residual protein.⁠37–39 Accordingly, patients with truncating variants on both alleles usually have no detectable laminin-α2 in muscle and classical LAMA2-CMD.5,36,40,41 Conversely, in a Russian series, 4 of 7 patients with milder disease and none of 83 with the classical form carried a missense variant.⁠42

The LG domains of laminin-211 bind integrin α7β1 and dystro­ glycan (Fig. 2B), which is cleaved from a single precursor into extracellular α-DG and transmembrane β-DG.69 α-DG binds laminin through matriglycan, a polymer of xylose and glucuronic acid that the glycosyltransferase LARGE1 synthesizes on the mucin-like region of α-DG.70,71 α-DG binds laminin-α2 at LG4-5 and also at LG1-3 (ref. 10). Laminins bind integrins through LG1-3 and the C-terminal tail of γ1 (refs. 9,72). In crystals of LG4-5 with a matriglycan oligosaccharide, a single disaccharide chelates the calcium ion of LG4. Affinity nevertheless rose with chain length, from a dissociation constant (KD) of 3.7 µM for three sugars to 0.23 µM for five sugars, and long chains were predicted to favor rebinding.⁠73 Accordingly, mouse muscle with only short matriglycan bound laminin but was not protected from eccentric contraction.⁠74 This finding suggests that a link able to withstand contraction depends on avidity, that is, on multiple binding sites that allow laminin to rebind along a long matriglycan chain. The same requirement may apply to an antibody that substitutes for this link.

Animal models indicate that a laminin-α2-deficient fiber fails by detaching from the surrounding matrix, whereas a dystrophin-deficient fiber fails when its sarcolemma becomes permeable. Evans blue, a dye that normal fibers exclude, labeled groups of fibers in mdx mice, which lack dystrophin, but only occasional single fibers in hypomorphic dy/dy and dy2J mice.⁠81 Failure of laminin-α2-deficient fibers has been imaged only in zebrafish, in which loaded fibers of the lama2 mutant candyfloss detached from the myoseptum with membranes still imperme­ able and underwent apoptosis later (Fig. 2D).⁠59 Sparing of the sarcolemma is unexpected, because loss of dystroglycan or matriglycan renders it fragile.⁠78 We suspect that other ligands of α-DG, such as perlecan, still occupy matriglycan,⁠10,14 in which case the plane of failure would lie within the basement membrane and the DGC would remain bound to the matrix. The sarcolemma and the DGC of a laminin-α2-deficient fiber would thus remain intact until the fiber detaches. Restoring the connection between the DGC and the remaining laminin would therefore address the primary defect of the disease.

Cell death is the earliest postnatal event recorded after loss of laminin-α2 (Fig. 2D). In dy3K mice, the most severely affected strain,⁠56 limb muscle was normal at embryonic day 18.5 and contained apoptotic, caspase-3-positive cells on postnatal day 1, and degenerating fibers, mononuclear infiltrates and collagen III deposits were present by day 4 (ref. 60). Inflammation and matrix deposition thus followed cell death and are most simply explained as responses to it. By week 4, the tibialis anterior of hypomorphic dyW mice82 contained one third of the normal num­ ber of fibers.⁠61 Regeneration also requires laminin-α2, because injured muscle of dyW mice rarely completed repair unless the mice carried a LAMA2 transgene.⁠62

The cytoplasmic tail of β-DG binds dystrophin,⁠11 and along with the sarcoglycans and sarcospan, these proteins form the dys­ trophin-glycoprotein complex (DGC).⁠75 Cryo-electron microscopy maps of the native DGC76,77 suggest how load is transmitted through the complex. The cleavage site between α- and β-DG lies within a single folded domain to which both subunits contribute, and a flexible linker tethers this domain to the transmembrane helix of β-DG. The adjoining immunoglobulin-like domain of α-DG attaches to the edge of a tower, about 13.5 nm tall, formed by β-, γ- and δ-sarcoglycan. Load on matriglycan must therefore be transmitted along the mucin-like region to the immunoglobulin- like domain, where it could divide between the tower and the tether of β-DG.

The downstream processes in muscle follow from the loss of a single extracellular connection, as restoration of laminin-α2 to the muscle of dyW and dy2J mice with a LAMA2 transgene greatly improved morphology and longevity.⁠83 These mice nevertheless remained lame in the hindlimbs, most likely because Schwann cells also depend on laminin-α2, whose loss results in partial amyelination of spinal roots.⁠84 This peripheral neuropathy is a feature of the mouse models. In patients, it rarely manifests clini­ cally, and its contribution to weakness is considered minimal.⁠5,56 Persistent hindlimb lameness in mice that receive a muscle- directed treatment therefore reflects disease of the nerve and has little counterpart in patients.

Laminin-411 in laminin-α2-deficient muscle

Although dystroglycan and integrin α7 both contributed to force production in mouse muscle, only the loss of dystroglycan detached the basement membrane from the sarcolemma and rendered mice prone to contraction-induced injury.⁠78 The muscle of Largemyd mice, in which the DGC is intact but matriglycan is missing, displays the same defects.⁠78 Protection from injury therefore depends on the bond between matriglycan and the basement membrane. A laminin-α1 transgene without LG4-5, the sole dystroglycan site of laminin-α1, corrected diaphragm and heart of laminin-α2-null dy3K mice, whereas limb muscles degenerated.⁠79 Polymerization and integrin binding, which this truncated chain retains, thus suffice in the diaphragm and heart, but limb skeletal muscle uniquely requires the link to dystro­ glycan.

Expression and function of laminin-α4

Consequences of laminin-α2 deficiency

Without laminin-α2, the β1 and γ1 chains persist, predominantly assembled with laminin-α4 to form laminin-411. Laminin-411 forms little or no polymer and is poorly anchored to the sarcolemma (Fig. 2C).⁠14,15 Consistent with these properties, the basement membrane of the muscle fibers was severely disrupted on electron microscopy in three patients (Fig. 2D).⁠12 The two laminin receptors are affected differently by the loss of their ligand. Laminin-α4 is normally a component of vascular, neural and synaptic basement membranes. All endothelial cells produce laminin-411 (ref. 85), and Lama4-null mice have perinatal hemor­ rhages and discontinuous capillary basement membranes, which indicates a role in the maturation of microvessels.⁠86 In peripheral nerve, laminin-411 accompanies laminin-211 in the basement membrane of Schwann cells, and its loss impairs the sorting and myelination of axons.⁠87,88 At the neuromuscular junction, laminin-421 (α4β2γ1) aligns the active zones of the nerve terminal with the folds of the postsynaptic membrane.⁠89 Laminin-α4 also surrounds developing muscle fibers,⁠7,86 but in adult muscle it is very low or undetectable outside vessels and synapses.⁠15 An anti-laminin-α4 antibody will therefore also encounter its target in vessels, nerves and synapses.

Polymerization and receptor binding

Mini-agrin, alone or together with αLNNd, was also effective when first supplied after birth. Mini-agrin delivered alone by AAV on day 2 or 3 extended mean lifespan of dyW mice from 10.2 to 42.1 weeks (ref. 100). αLNNd was shortened to fit one AAV genome together with the ubiquitous promoter needed to treat the peripheral neuropathy of the mice,⁠23,24 and both linkers delivered by AAV at 3 weeks of age improved grip strength and reduced fibrosis.⁠24 When a doxycycline-regulated mini-agrin transgene was induced at about day 28, survival rose 2- to 3-fold, but motor performance improved more with earlier onset and existing fibrosis was not reversed.⁠98

Laminin-211 and laminin-411 differ only in the α chain (Fig. 3A). Human laminin-α2 (3,122 residues) carries a full short arm capped by an LN domain, whereas in laminin-α4 (1,823 residues) this arm is truncated to EGF-like modules.⁠43,49 Because a polymer node requires an α LN domain to stabilize the bond between the β1 and γ1 LN domains,⁠8 laminin-411 alone is not expected to polymerize (Fig. 3B). Accordingly, it has been shown to form little to no polymer in in vitro sedimentation assays.⁠15

An antibody that bridges LG4-5 of laminin-α4 to β-DG would add no polymerizing arm, and mini-agrin is therefore the linker closest in mechanism to such a bispecific bridging antibody. In dyW mice, mini-agrin alone partly restored basement membrane structure and stable binding of β1γ1 but did not change the extractability of laminin-α4 (ref. 15). Because mini-agrin binds γ1, it also anchors laminin-511, which may account for part of its benefit.⁠15 No linker binds LG4-5 of laminin-α4, a segment that is released by proteolysis in cell culture and was not detected in normal tissues.⁠20 Because a bispecific bridging antibody would depend on this segment, we examined whether it is retained in the muscle of dyW mice (Results).

Other approaches

The LG domains of laminin-α4 are also poor ligands for the receptors of the fiber (Fig. 3A). Recombinant LG fragments of laminin-α4 have 30- to 100-fold lower affinity for α-DG than LG1-3 of laminin-α2 (ref. 20), and full-length laminin-411 exhibits minimal binding to integrin α7β1 or α-DG,⁠15 the latter of which may be explained by the absence of a Ca2+ site. In the crystal structure of LG4-5 of laminin-α2, matriglycan binds through the Ca2+ ion of LG4 (ref. 73), and no equivalent site is predicted from the laminin-α4 sequence.⁠43,97 When we superposed LG4 of the AlphaFold model of human laminin-α4 (ref. 92) on this mouse structure, laminin-α4 residues of opposite charge aligned with Asp2876, one of two aspartates that bind the ion, and with Arg2803, on which the xylose stacks (Fig. 3C). Binding to integrin α7β1 is limited by splicing, because only the X1 variant bound laminin-411 (ref. 9), whereas adult mouse muscle expresses X2 (ref. 94). Consistent with these properties, sequential extraction of muscle released laminin-α4 of patients with collagenase, whereas laminin-α2 of controls was released by EDTA, which dissolves the laminin polymer and Ca2+-dependent receptor bonds.⁠10,14,15 Laminin-411 is thus only loosely attached, being neither poly­ merized nor firmly anchored to the fiber (Fig. 3D), and it does not functionally compensate for laminin-211.

Therapeutic approaches for LAMA2-CMD

Linker proteins

Direct replacement of LAMA2 would restore polymerization and anchorage at once but is hindered by size, because its coding sequence spans about 9.4 kb and an AAV vector accommodates about 4.7 kb (Fig. 4A).⁠23,101 Although the rod domain of dystrophin can be shortened,⁠102 laminin-α2 offers no comparably dispens­ able region.⁠24,100 Its LN domain, coiled coil and LG domains together account for about 5.5 kb,⁠49 which exceeds the capacity of one vector. Direct correction of LAMA2 by gene editing is likewise hindered by mutational heterogeneity, because truncating and splice-site variants are distributed throughout the gene without clear hotspots and only 14 of the 309 disease-associated variants in the international database had 10 or more independent entries.⁠36 Vectors have thus far restored full-length laminin-α2 only in dy2J mice, a mild model,⁠15 either from three AAV genomes whose products are joined by split inteins25 or through Cas9 repair of the splice-site variant specific to this strain.⁠103 Like linker proteins, the other strategies that address the primary defect in mice substitute for laminin-α2 (Fig. 4C).

Because laminin-411 is abundant in laminin-α2-deficient muscle but lacks polymerization and anchorage, two linker proteins were engineered to supply these functions (Fig. 3A, E). Both bind its γ1 chain, at the sites that nidogen occupies on the short arm and agrin on the coiled coil.⁠95,96 Mini-agrin joins the laminin- binding N-terminal domain of agrin to its LG domains,⁠22,98 which bind matriglycan on α-DG.29 In vitro, mini-agrin increased the binding of laminin-411 to α-DG (Fig. 3D), and when expressed from a muscle-specific transgene it raised the median survival of dyW mice from 15.5 to 50 weeks (ref. 15). The transgene also raised tetanic force of the extensor digitorum longus to 4-fold that of untreated dyW mice, or 37% of control.⁠99

Laminin-α1 is the laminin chain most closely related to laminin-α2 (ref. 28). It is expressed in early embryonic muscle as laminin-111, which laminin-211 replaces during develop­ ment, and LAMA1 remains silent in muscle after birth.⁠26–28 When expressed from a transgene, laminin-α1 largely replaced laminin-α2 in dy3K mice.⁠117 Because LAMA1 also exceeds the capacity of AAV, vectors instead deliver catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9) fused to transcriptional activators, which reactivate the endogenous gene (Fig. 4B).⁠26 However, activation appears to be inefficient. Uniform staining for laminin-α1 in dy2J mice required two vectors at a combined dose of 6 × 1014 vector genomes per kg.⁠26 In juvenile non-human primates, a single vector at 1 × 1014 vector genomes per kg raised LAMA1 mRNA in muscle to 3% to 6% of the level of the housekeeping gene HPRT.⁠27

The second linker, αLNNd, fuses the LN-bearing end of laminin-α1 to the laminin-binding part of nidogen-1 (ref. 21) and supplies polymerization, which mini-agrin does not restore. Potential role for a bispecific bridging antibody

We chose dystroglycan as the membrane anchor because it alone among the laminin receptors keeps the basement mem­ brane attached to the sarcolemma78 and appears to persist without laminin-α2 (Fig. 2C). We targeted its transmembrane subunit, β-DG, as in the published study in α-dystroglycanopa­ thy mice (Fig. 5B),⁠30 so that force on the β-DG ectodomain is transmitted through the cytoplasmic tail to dystrophin. By com­ parison, α-DG would be a less dependable anchor, because it is often secondarily lost30,131 or heterogeneously glycosylated29,132 in dystrophic muscle (Fig. 5C). Since the binding of an anti-β-DG antibody is matriglycan-independent, the same arm could also serve in the α-dystroglycanopathies.

The laminin arm targets LG4-5 of laminin-α4, the counterpart of the domain pair through which laminin-α2 binds matrigly­ can,⁠73 on the working assumption that an antibody bound to LG4-5 would hold laminin-411 as α-DG holds laminin-211. LG4-5 also contains the epitope of a published anti-laminin-α4 antibody that supplied the variable regions for our tool molecules (Methods).

An antibody that bridges laminin-411 to β-dystroglycan follows the principle of mini-agrin but would be given by subcutaneous or intravenous injection rather than expressed from a vector (Fig. 4B). Linkers injected as proteins would bind matrix proteins that are not specific to the muscle basement membrane.⁠15 We reasoned that an arm targeting laminin-α4, which in normal adult muscle is confined to capillaries, perineurium and the synaptic cleft,⁠7 would encounter a smaller antigen sink than the linker proteins, which bind the γ1 chain that laminin-411 shares with other laminins. Such a bispecific bridging antibody would restore anchorage irrespective of the LAMA2 variant, provided that laminin-411 is present.⁠24 In fact, such an approach has been explored in related muscular dystrophies. A bispecific bridging antibody targeting laminin-211 and β-dystroglycan improved muscle function in a mouse model of α-dystroglycanopathy.⁠30

The reach and valency of the antibody and the local density of its targets matter as much as its affinity. The epitope reported for the β-DG arm lies within a few nanometers of the cell mem­ brane (Methods), beside the sarcoglycan tower of about 13.5 nm (refs. 76,77). Because the antigen-binding sites of an IgG lie ~12– 13 nm apart,⁠133 the laminin arm of a 1+1 bispecific (one arm per target) would extend only slightly beyond this tower, and binding probably relies on the flexible linker that tethers LG4-5 to LG1-3 (ref. 97).

From a translational perspective, exposure to an injected anti­ body can be measured and adjusted, whereas a vector is given once at a fixed dose.⁠119 Dose and interval can be titrated against plasma concentration and response, as shown for adalimumab in children,⁠121 and adapted to growth.⁠122 The distribution of IgG is also predictable. Its concentration in muscle is about 4% of that in plasma, and such ratios predicted tissue concentrations in mice, rats, monkeys and humans within 2-fold.116 Accordingly, the published bispecific bridging antibody was detected in limb muscles, diaphragm and heart of wild-type mice after a single intravenous dose.⁠30 By comparison, most genomes of an intravenous AAV9 vector accumulated in the liver of dy2J mice.⁠26 Whether an antibody reaches muscles and fibers more uniformly than a vector has not been tested thus far.

Valency matters because a single bridge is short-lived. By surface plasmon resonance (SPR), the tool arms release β-DG with half-lives of 4 to 9 min and laminin-α4 over tens of minutes (Results), and mechanical load shortens the lifetime of such bonds further.⁠134 A fiber can therefore remain attached only through many bridges in parallel, each of which reforms soon after it breaks,⁠135 as proposed for the bond between matriglycan and laminin-211 (ref. 74). A second arm for the same target should favor this, because it holds the antibody in place while the released arm rebinds. This reasoning led us to compare formats from 1+1 to 2+2 that carry the same arms.

Proof of principle for this mechanism of action comes from the α-dystroglycanopathies (Fig. 5B). In these disorders, variants in at least 18 genes leave matriglycan short or absent,⁠136 and α-DG then holds laminin-211 too weakly to withstand load, or not at all.⁠29,74 By contrast, β-DG is retained in the muscle of Fukuyama CMD patients and Largemyd-3J mice,⁠30,128 and laminin-α2 is present (Fig. 5C). Because laminin-211 polymerizes,⁠93 it needs only to be anchored. An antibody targeting β-DG and LG4-5 of laminin-α2 would bypass matriglycan, whichever glycosylation gene is affected, so these disorders test directly whether an antibody can replace the missing link.

For patients and physicians, an antibody would differ from a vector chiefly in that treatment can be stopped (Fig. 4C). Intra­ venous AAV at 1014 vector genomes per kg or more, the range of the doses cited above, has been followed by fatal liver failure in X-linked myotubular myopathy123 and Duchenne muscular dystrophy,⁠124 by a fatal innate immune reaction104 and by throm­ botic microangiopathy.⁠125 Such outcomes are rare,⁠126 but a vector cannot be withdrawn, and recipients receive corticosteroids for one to several months.⁠127 In contrast, an antibody can be interrupted if it is not tolerated, its effect is reversible,⁠30 and gene therapy remains available afterward, because no anti-capsid antibodies are induced. We consider this flexibility valuable in LAMA2-CMD, in which more than half of the children with complete deficiency were admitted to the hospital for respiratory complications within their first two years.⁠3 The safety of a bispe­ cific bridging antibody itself remains to be established.

Therapeutic hypothesis

Such an antibody improved muscle function in adult Largemyd-3J mice, which received a tandem bispecific immunoglobulin with two sites per target at 30 mg/kg twice weekly for 7 weeks (ref. 30). Grip strength reached nearly 70% of wild type, treadmill time doubled and fewer tibialis anterior fibers took up Evans blue. An antibody can thus partly replace the link between laminin and β-DG in living muscle.

We therefore propose that a bispecific bridging antibody com­ prised of binding arms targeting LG4-5 of laminin-α4 and the ectodomain of β-DG would behave like a laminin-α2 mimetic, bridging laminin-411 to the DGC and restoring basement mem­ brane anchorage (Fig. 5A). The second molecule is a humanized 2+2 IgG-scFv, an anti-β-DG IgG with a disulfide-stapled anti-LAMA4 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) fused to the C terminus of each heavy chain,⁠140,141 and as a homodimer it requires neither knobs-into-holes nor a crossover. It belongs to a series that varies the number and position of the arms for each target, in which both arms are humanized and the human IgG1 Fc carries LALA-PG. We built this series because β-DG in the sarcolemma and laminin-411 in the basement membrane are both surface-bound targets, for which a second arm of the same specificity could add avidity. The 2+2 was one of two humanized designs that supported strong adhesion (Supplementary Fig. S1D) in the assay described below, in which an antibody bridges dystroglycan-overexpressing cells to immobilized LAMA4 LG4-5. We therefore advanced it to dyW mice despite its human sequences.

A single humanized arm appears to bind β-DG far more weakly on cells than in solution. Both h1+1 curves rose in proportion to concentration up to 138 nM. This concentration exceeds the KD by more than 30-fold and would nearly saturate a binding site of that affinity. The continued rise cannot be attributed to dissociation during the washes, which would lower a curve without delaying its saturation. Because the soluble antigen bound in SPR also contains α-DG (Methods), the obstacle on cells is more plausibly the adjacent cell membrane than α-DG. Given a single contact this weak, avidity is the simplest explanation for the difference between constructs with one and two N-terminal β-DG arms (Fig. 7C). The two Fabs of an IgG can engage antigens 3 to 17 nm apart,⁠142 a spacing that overexpressing cells are likely to offer, and we attribute the 18- to 35-fold shift between the h1+1 CrossMabs and the 2+2 to such a second contact.

Both tool molecules bound β-DG and LAMA4 with KD values of 0.5 to 4 nM, as measured by SPR with captured antibody and soluble antigen (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Table S1). Each arm of the 2+2 bound within 2-fold of the same arm in an IgG or a 1+1 CrossMab, so the 2+2 format preserved affinity. For both molecules, the complex with β-DG dissociated faster than that with LAMA4, so that the membrane end of a bridge should open first. As Fabs, the humanized arms bound the murine antigens about as well as the human antigens or better (Methods), which supports the use of the 2+2 in mice.

The 1+1 CrossMab engaged both antigens simultaneously, as bridging requires. In a dual-antigen ELISA, which captures the antibody on plate-bound LAMA4 and detects it with biotinylated β-DG ectodomain, it gave a saturable signal (Fig. 6D). The 2+2 was not measured in this ELISA, and its simultaneous binding is inferred from its activity in the adhesion assay.

We propose that access to β-DG may be sterically hindered for an arm below the Fc (Fig. 7C), because avidity does not explain the weak staining by the 1+2. The 1+2 also has one β-DG arm but needed 36- to 82-fold more antibody than the h1+1 CrossMabs to reach an MFI of 10,000. The crossover of the constant domains (CH1-CL) in the β-DG Fab of the 1+2 may add to the effect of its position. Consistent with hindered access, each arm below the Fc had a lower on-rate in SPR (Supplementary Table S2) than the same arm at the N terminus. The β-DG Fab of the 1+2 is joined to the Fc by its paratope end and can reach its epitope only if the Fc approaches the cell membrane. Together, these data suggest that humanized arms engage membrane-bound β-DG best when presented as two N-terminal Fabs.

Binding to membrane-bound β-dystroglycan

Because flow cytometry measures only one side of the bridge (Fig. 7), we used a cell adhesion assay to test whether the same constructs can hold cells on a surface coated with LAMA4 (Fig. 8A, B). Wells coated with the LG4-5 fragment of human LAMA4 (the LG4-5 coat) were incubated with antibody and washed before dystroglycan-overexpressing cells were added. Cells that with­ stood two centrifugal washes were quantified and expressed as a percentage of the positive control (poly-L-lysine, PLL). A cell remains attached only if enough bridges share the force of the wash.⁠135,143 We therefore take the EC50, the concentration that gave half-maximal retention, to reflect the functional potency of each antibody.

The affinities above were measured by SPR, which presents β-DG as a soluble protein, whereas a bispecific bridging antibody encounters membrane-bound β-DG. On cells, part of the β-DG ectodomain is folded together with α-DG,⁠76,77 and the epitope published for the β-DG arm (Fig. 6B) lies in a poorly ordered stretch next to the cell membrane. Because access to this epitope may depend on antibody format, we compared five constructs carrying the same humanized arms in different numbers and positions, in solution and on cells. Two are humanized 1+1 CrossMabs (h1+1), with the VH-VL crossover in the LAMA4 or the β-DG Fab. The 2+1 and the 2+2 are anti-β-DG IgGs with one or two LAMA4 arms, each fused to a heavy-chain C terminus below the Fc, and the 1+2 is an anti-LAMA4 IgG with a single crossed β-DG Fab in the same position.

Both tool molecules bridged cells to the LG4-5 coat, and the murine 1+1 CrossMab retained them as fully as the 2+2 (Fig. 8C; Supplementary Table S3). Its EC50 values of 7.67 to 32.9 nM, com­ pared with 2.40 to 9.69 nM for the 2+2 (Fig. 8C), suggest that the 2+2 is more potent. However, no plate held both tool molecules, and EC50 values from different days could be distinguished only when they differed by more than about 5-fold (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B).

Pharmacokinetics and muscle exposure

To bridge β-DG to laminin-α4 in vivo, a dosed antibody must leave the blood, reach the surface of the muscle fiber and persist there between doses (Fig. 9A). We therefore interrogated the plasma pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of our tool molecules across wild-type and dyW mice.

Because the concentration required in vivo also depends on the abundance of the two targets, we estimated both from pub­ lished protein abundances, assuming one β-DG per dystrophin (Fig. 9F, Methods). Dystrophin-bound β-DG was estimated at 15 to 20 nM and laminin-411 in laminin-α2-deficient muscle at 120 to 170 nM. At about 30 nM (50 mg/kg weekly) and 60 nM (72 mg/ kg twice weekly), the 1+1 CrossMab lay between the two, the range in which a bridging molecule with high-affinity arms is predicted to form the most bridges.⁠146 These estimates suggest that, in dyW mice, the doses administered may have supplied the 1+1 CrossMab in molar excess over dystrophin-bound β-DG without reaching the surplus at which antibody caps both targets separately and bridging declines (hook effect). A systemically dosed bispecific bridging antibody can therefore be recovered from dystrophic muscle days after last dose, retains the ability to bind both antigens, and is present at concentrations exceeding those required for bridging in vitro. Delivery to muscle is thus unlikely to be a fundamental or existential constraint for the application of bispecific bridging antibodies in muscle-related diseases.

Functional translation in a LAMA2-CMD animal model

Wild-type mice received one intravenous dose of the 2+2 IgG- scFv (25 or 50 mg/kg) or of either parental IgG (anti-β-DG or anti-LAMA4; 25 mg/kg), after which total human IgG in plasma was followed for 28 days (Fig. 9B). The 2+2 was eliminated from plasma faster than either monospecific parental IgG. Its effective half-life was about 30 h, compared with 202 and 57 h for the anti-β-DG and anti-LAMA4 IgGs, respectively (Fig. 9C, Supple­ mentary Fig. S3A). At equal mass dose (25 mg/kg), it was cleared 3.9- and 1.7-fold faster than these two IgGs, respectively (Supple­ mentary Table S4). Moreover, exposure to the 2+2 increased less than in proportion to dose (Supplementary Fig. S3B), which suggests that the doses administered may have been too low to saturate its clearance. Plasma exposure to the 2+2 therefore lasts days rather than weeks in wild-type mice. Wild-type mice, however, do not share the laminin-α4 expression pattern of dyW mice, and we have not characterized the extent to which this influences pharmacokinetics.

The dyW mouse is the workhorse model of the LAMA2-CMD field.⁠147 Linker proteins and laminin-111 protein were evaluated in it,⁠15,28,100 and its phenotype has been compared among labo­ ratories to standardize preclinical testing.⁠147 To test whether a bispecific bridging antibody improves muscle function, we chose this model, because it is deficient in laminin-α2, retains laminin-α4 around its fibers and lives long enough for several weeks of dosing.⁠15 The dyW mouse shares early onset, failure to thrive, inflammation and fibrosis with severe LAMA2-CMD (Fig. 10A, B).⁠5,56 The disease begins before birth, with small muscles at embryonic day 18.5 (ref. 148), growth fails from week 3 (ref. 61), and at 6 to 7 weeks our vehicle-treated dyW mice weighed half as much as wild-type mice. Even dosing from birth, therefore, begins after disease onset, and the severe course leaves a short window in which to detect a benefit.

The peripheral neuropathy of dyW mice, in contrast, is a feature of the mouse models that rarely manifests clinically in patients.⁠5,56 Because hindlimb paralysis was not prevented when laminin function was restored in muscle alone,⁠15,24,83 we expected the neuropathy to persist under antibody treatment (Fig. 4C) and, as a result, prioritized forelimb-centric functional measures.

Among the other endpoints, grip strength per body mass was higher in the 2+2 group than in the vehicle group at weeks 4 and 6 (week 6, 1.94-fold, 1.43 to 2.63; Fig. 11C), as was absolute grip strength at week 6 (2.13-fold, 1.64 to 2.78; Supplementary Table S7). However, absolute grip strength also rose 2.65-fold in the wild-type mice (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Body mass of the dyW mice remained about half that of wild-type mice, irrespective of treatment (Fig. 11D).

Groups were then compared by peak force, the largest response of each mouse (Methods). The 2+2 group developed 1.95-fold the peak force of the vehicle group (95% CI 1.51 to 2.51; n = 7 and 4; Fig. 11B, Supplementary Table S7). Because each litter received a single treatment, we repeated the comparison on litter means,⁠150 which gave the same ratio and P = 0.057 by the exact rank test. This difference equals 39.5% of the deficit of the vehicle group relative to wild-type mice. By comparison, mice given the positive control, AAV9 mini-agrin, developed 1.31-fold the peak force of the vehicle group (1.00 to 1.72). These data suggest a partial restoration of force following bispecific antibody treatment.

Overall, both measures of forelimb function, evoked triceps force and grip strength, were about twice as high in the 2+2 IgG-scFv group as in the vehicle group. Because the paralysis of dyW mice is confined to the hindlimbs, the forelimbs are the limbs in which a gain in muscle function can be measured. We take this consistency across functional measures as in vivo proof of principle for the therapeutic approach. These results set a strong foundation for testing and optimizing novel, proprietary bispecific bridging antibodies.

Working with Claude

We next compared the two tool molecules at the same nominal molar dose (Fig. 10C). Mice given the 1+1 CrossMab did not differ detectably in peak force from the vehicle group (1.08-fold, 0.67 to 1.76; Fig. 11B), while the 2+2 IgG-scFv group developed 1.80-fold their peak force (1.12 to 2.91; Supplementary Fig. S4B). Weekly regimens of the 1+1 CrossMab at lower doses likewise showed no detectable benefit (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). Because this antibody was recovered from muscle (Fig. 9D), the lack of a detectable benefit may reflect insufficient bridging rather than insufficient delivery, consistent with the higher apparent potency of the 2+2 in vitro.

Discussion

On day 4, the production CRO delivered its data package, a report of 341 pages, which one of us forwarded to Claude in two sentences, with the request to review it, share observations and recommend next steps. Claude returned a bottom line after 2 min 40 s and, once it had read every page, a visual synthesis of all 113 constructs after 7 min 53 s (Fig. 12B). The synthesis arranged the constructs on the grid of 14 by 8 arms. Claude marked 82 constructs for screening as is, seven for additional polishing, and the 24 constructs of three laminin-binding arms to be held back from the cell-based assays on production criteria (Fig. 12D). Each of the 113 constructs thus had a proposed next step in less than 8 min.

We hypothesized that a bispecific bridging antibody targeting β-DG and LG4-5 of laminin-α4 would act as a laminin-α2 mimetic, bridging laminin-411 to the sarcolemma and restoring anchor­ age in laminin-α2-deficient muscle fibers. We designed and characterized two tool molecules, a murine 1+1 CrossMab and a humanized 2+2 IgG-scFv, which bound their respective targets with nanomolar affinity and facilitated cellular adhesion in vitro. In dyW mice, treatment with the 2+2 IgG-scFv was associated with about twice the evoked forelimb force and grip strength of vehicle-treated mice. We regard this functional activity as proof of principle for the therapeutic approach.

Four minutes later, Claude had revised its memo, the working page, the run order and three unsent drafts from 113 to 89 constructs. It had also applied the ruling to three dependent steps in the unsent drafts, each flagged for our veto (Fig. 12D). After the ruling, eight emails drafted by Claude went out on the same day to three CROs and a consultant.

Bridging in vivo requires delivery to muscle, which is unlikely to be a fundamental constraint. In dyW mice, the 1+1 CrossMab recovered from muscle days after last dose bound both antigens, and its mean concentration exceeded the KD of either arm and its EC50 in the adhesion assay (Fig. 9D). Immunofluorescence was consistent with its localization at the sarcolemma (Fig. 9E). Moreover, antibody may reside longer in muscle than in plasma, because the published antibody targeting laminin-211 and β-DG was detected in perfused muscle for up to 14 days, despite a serum half-life of about 30 h (ref. 30). The 2+2 IgG-scFv had a similar half- life in wild-type mice and was cleared faster than its parental IgGs (Fig. 9B, C), and binding to laminin-α4 is likely the limiting variable for its exposure.

The 2+2, which appeared more potent than the 1+1 CrossMab in vitro, was also associated with about twice the peak triceps force and grip strength of vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 11B, C), whereas a nominally equimolar dose of the 1+1 CrossMab had no detectable effect on force (Fig. 11B). Anchorage without polymerization

Limitations

Laminin-211 has two functions, polymerization and anchorage, and a bispecific bridging antibody only restores the latter. Evidence from human genetics and animal models suggests anchorage alone can deliver disease-modifying effects. Patients with missense variants in the LN domain, which impair polymer­ ization, usually walk, whereas variants in the LG domains cause severe congenital disease despite the presence of laminin-α2 (refs. 5,36,47). Normal muscle function thus appears to depend more on anchorage than polymerization. In mice, mini-agrin is the closest precedent, and it alone more than triples the lifespan of dyW mice.⁠15

The main limitations concern the pilot study, in which groups were enrolled in sequence, vehicle followed a different regimen (Fig. 10C) and each litter received one treatment without random­ ization. Statistical support therefore depends on whether mice or litters are the unit of analysis (Fig. 11, Methods), and litter means gave P = 0.057, the smallest value possible with seven litters. Losses before week 6 left four to seven treated mice per group (Fig. 10C), so groups may differ by survival as well as by treatment. Because six treated groups (Supplementary Table S6) and at least 11 endpoints were examined, a chance finding is possible.

We suspect that anchorage acts largely through assembly of the basement membrane. Mini-agrin partly restored the structure of this membrane,⁠15 and the sarcolemma is largely spared in laminin-α2-deficient mice.⁠81 However, mini-agrin did not change the extractability of laminin-α4 (ref. 15), and direct evidence that laminin-411 becomes linked to nidogen and collagen IV is lacking. If anchorage acts through assembly, a bridge would need to hold laminin-411 at the sarcolemma densely enough for these links to form.

A bispecific bridging antibody may anchor laminin-411 more effectively than mini-agrin, for several reasons. First, the anti­ body binds β-DG directly, whereas mini-agrin binds matriglycan on α-DG.29,98 The bridge is therefore independent of the state of α-DG in dystrophic muscle (Fig. 5C), and it leaves matriglycan free for perlecan and other ligands.⁠10,14 Second, the antibody holds laminin-411 at LG4-5, the counterpart of the domains through which laminin-211 is anchored,⁠73 whereas mini-agrin binds the coiled coil of the long arm.⁠95 Third, the affinity, valency and geometry of an antibody can be engineered. Among constructs that share arms and Fc, format alone raised cell retention from at most 20% to 78 and 88% of the positive control (Supplementary Fig. S1D), and bridging was most efficient with two β-DG Fabs. Fourth, exposure to an antibody can be raised until its targets are saturated, whereas a vector is given once at a fixed dose.⁠119 Consistent with these arguments, peak force was 1.95-fold that of vehicle-treated mice in the 2+2 IgG-scFv group and 1.31-fold in the AAV9 mini-agrin group (Fig. 11B).

Exposure to the 2+2 cannot be related to its effect, because it was measured only in the plasma of wild-type mice, and muscle levels were measured only for the 1+1 CrossMab (Fig. 6E). Neither the in­ tact 2+2 nor the anti-LAMA4 IgG, whose re-humanized arm differs from that of the 2+2, was tested on murine antigens, and the per­ sistence of the 2+2 in muscle over the 6 or 7 days between last dose and force test was not measured (Methods). The pharmacokinetic comparison confounds format, Fc substitutions, target binding and anti-drug antibodies, which were not measured in any study, and it does not explain why exposure to the 2+2 increased less than in proportion to dose. The 1+1 CrossMab recovered from muscle may have been unbound, because these muscles were not perfused and plasma was not measured. Whole muscle holds about 4% of the plasma concentration of a non-binding IgG,⁠116 so about 200 µg/mL in plasma, which a conventional IgG could reach on this regimen (Fig. 9B), would account for the mean levels in muscle. The stoichiometric estimate (Fig. 9F) compares whole- tissue totals and does not measure occupancy. It is uncertain by more than an order of magnitude, because published amounts of dystrophin correspond to 2 to 730 nM (refs. 152,153). At the 466 nM reported for mouse muscle,⁠154 the 1+1 CrossMab would match only about one eighth of dystrophin-bound β-DG, which would then exceed laminin-411. A molar excess also need not result in occupancy, because a single humanized Fab engaged membrane- bound β-DG only weakly (Fig. 7B) and laminin-α4 outside the basement membrane of the fiber7,14 may hold antibody out of its reach.

The in vitro assays described here are simplified systems that lack several controls. The adhesion assay, with dystroglycan- overexpressing cells on a fragment of laminin-α4, reproduces neither repeated stress nor the native density of β-DG. Arms below the Fc had lower on-rates than at the N terminus, which may in part reflect capture of the antibody through its Fc in SPR. Most comparisons of humanized formats rest on a single experiment with one lot, so we interpret only large differences.

The antibody carries no LN domain and would therefore leave laminin-411 unpolymerized. Patients whose variants impair only polymerization would not be expected to benefit,⁠23 and nerve and brain would remain untreated, because IgG enters both poorly.⁠115,116 The fullest rescue of dyW mice required both mini-agrin and αLNNd.⁠15 In these mice, αLNNd also prevented hindlimb paralysis, a feature of the mouse models that rarely manifests clinically in patients,⁠5,56 whereas ubiquitously ex­ pressed mini-agrin did not.⁠24 The antibody could therefore be combined with a single AAV vector for αLNNd.⁠23

α-Dystroglycanopathies

Both targets also remain incompletely characterized in dis­ eased muscle. The density of laminin-411 on the fibers of patients has not been measured, so the number of bridges that an antibody could form is unknown. Evidence that β-DG persists in patients rests on two cases with residual laminin-α2 (ref. 32). Safety was not addressed, although both targets are expressed in tissues well beyond the muscle fiber.⁠7,155

Conclusion

The same β-DG arm and format could serve the α-dystrogly­ canopathies, for which fewer questions are open than for LAMA2-CMD. Laminin-211 is largely retained and polymerized, so the missing function is anchorage, which a bridge would supply to muscle but not to brain or eye. LG4-5 of laminin-α2 is accessible, since antibodies targeting it stain unfixed muscle.⁠30 Staining for laminin-α2 and β-DG was, however, reduced in small biopsy series.⁠129,151 Because the placement of the arms was as important as their number in vitro, an anti-β-DG IgG

Structural models, illustrations and registry search

Formats and production. We report ten bispecific bridging antibodies made at the tool-molecule CRO (Supplementary Table S1), namely two murine 1+1 CrossMabs on mouse IgG2a LALA-PG and eight humanized constructs on human IgG1/κ LALA-PG with the half-life-extending mutations M252Y/S254T/ T256E (YTE).⁠159 In heterodimers, heavy chains were paired by knobs-into-holes and light chains by a VH-VL or CH1-CL crossover in one Fab. In the murine 1+1 constructs, the LAMA4 heavy chain carried the knob and the β-DG heavy chain the hole. All ten constructs and the parental IgGs were first expressed in CHO-K1 cells (20 mL; 1 mL for the anti-LAMA4 IgG) and purified on protein A and, except for the murine constructs and the anti-LAMA4 IgG, by SEC. A second construct of the 2+2, identical except that it lacks YTE (2+2 without YTE), was made likewise at the production CRO in CHO-S cells (200 mL). We omitted YTE from the construct dosed in dyW mice because Fc mutations of this kind had lowered serum concentrations of IgG in mice.⁠160 The first construct (2+2 with YTE) was used for SPR, flow cytometry, adhesion and PK in wild-type mice, and the second for adhesion and dosing of dyW mice. Assembly was assessed as the share of the target mass in liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) of intact, denatured, deglycosylated protein, which cannot detect a swap of the two light chains (Supplementary Table S1).

Superposition of LG4 (Fig. 3C). LG4 of the AlphaFold model of human laminin-α4 (AF-Q16363-F1, version 4)92 was superposed on LG4 of mouse laminin-α2 (crystal structure, PDB 5IK8, mol­ ecule B)73 by iterative Cα-based structural alignment (custom script; 159 pairs within 4 Å, root-mean-square deviation 1.2 Å, 24% identity over 170 aligned residues). This alignment and three others (super, cealign and align in PyMOL 3.2) paired Arg2803 of laminin-α2 with Asp1513 of laminin-α4, and Asp2876 with Lys1587 (Ile1588 in cealign). Contacts were residues within 4.0 Å of the Ca2+ ion or the glycan. Tips of exposed side chains more than 10 Å from the ion were omitted from surfaces.

Antigens. Antigens were produced in CHO-K1 cells at the tool- molecule CRO, and their oligomeric state was not determined. Dosing. Antibodies and vehicle were injected intraperitoneally, at 5 mL/kg in the wild-type, vehicle and weekly 1+1 CrossMab groups and 6.77 mL/kg for the undiluted 2+2 (14.78 mg/mL). The study began with weekly injections, and we planned to move to twice-weekly dosing if early body mass did not improve. The 2+2 followed once it had been produced. By the schedule, twice- weekly regimens comprised 13 doses at 72 mg/kg and 12 of the 2+2. Terminal tests followed the last injection by 1 to 10 d (vehicle, 1 or 5; 2+2, 6 or 7), at 41 to 52 d of age (vehicle, 45 to 47; 2+2, 47). Choice of endpoints. Grip strength depends on motivation and operator.⁠171 Rotarod performance and plethysmography are less specific for skeletal muscle than grip strength and evoked triceps force, because the rotarod requires hindlimb stepping24 and quiet breathing draws on about one tenth of the pressure that a healthy diaphragm can generate.⁠172 Survival was not an endpoint, and histopathology was not assessed.

Treatment and testing of dyW mice

Grip strength. Mice held by the tail grasped a bar with the forepaws and were pulled horizontally until release (weeks 4 and 6). The score, in grams-force, was the mean of two sets of five pulls, each without its highest and lowest value. For Fig. 11C, it was divided by body mass, as entered with the test at week 4 and as measured at necropsy on the following day at week 6.

Study and groups. The pilot study began with weekly intraperi­ toneal injections of the 1+1 CrossMab, chosen to limit the handling of neonates. We later added two groups dosed twice weekly, first the 1+1 CrossMab at 72 mg/kg and then the 2+2 at 100 mg/kg. The main text reports these two groups and the three control groups (Fig. 10C), together with muscle levels in mice dosed weekly (Fig. 9D), and all groups are listed in Supplementary Table S6. Groups were enrolled and tested in sequence, because each litter received one treatment and the colony was small. As a result, the 2+2 group was tested last, 126 and 161 days after the two sessions of the vehicle group (Supplementary Fig. S4A), and the colony moved to a new facility in the interval.

Rotarod and plethysmography. After 3 d of acclimation (20 rpm, 5 min), mice ran on the rotarod (Ugo Basile) at 20 rpm for 3 min and then under acceleration until they fell (weeks 4 and 6; AAV9 mini-agrin, week 6 only). The rate of acceleration was not recorded. Minute volume (mL/min) was taken from unrestrained whole-body plethysmography (DSI; three periods of 2 min, week 6). Per gram of body mass, it did not differ between wild-type mice and dyW mice given vehicle (4.18 ± 1.12 and 5.07 ± 3.12 mL/ min/g; n = 12 and 4).

Animals. B6.129S1(Cg)-Lama2tm1Eeng/J mice (dyW; The Jackson Laboratory, stock 013786) were bred at the in vivo CRO from heterozygous pairs. Homozygotes and wild-type littermates or colony mates of both sexes were used (Supplementary Table S6). Mice were fed Teklad 2018 diet ad libitum, received moistened food and DietGel 76A daily and were weighed three to four times per week. The humane endpoint was the veterinarian’s judgment, and one mouse was euthanized as moribund. Concentrations from two single-dose studies (1+1 CrossMab, 15 dyW mice; 2+2, seven dyW and six wild-type mice) are not available.

Nerve-evoked triceps force. On the day after the tests without sedation and before necropsy, mice were anesthetized with isoflu­ rane and taped to a heated table. A pin was pressed against the humeral head, and the forelimb rested against a bar on the arm of a dual-mode lever (Aurora Scientific 305C). The radial nerve was stimulated through two percutaneous needle electrodes, with muscle and tendon left in place. The current was raised to the optimal isometric twitch (at least 30 s between twitches), and trains of 500 ms (0.2 ms pulses) followed at 1, 20, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100 and 150 Hz. Force is given in mN at the lever, without conversion to torque. Elbow angle, lever length and rest between trains were not recorded.

Data handling and use of Claude

Association with time. Between the two sessions of five wild- type mice, 61 d apart and at equal body mass, peak force changed 0.84-fold (0.61 to 1.16) and grip strength 2.65-fold (2.07 to 3.39; Supplementary Fig. S4C, D).
